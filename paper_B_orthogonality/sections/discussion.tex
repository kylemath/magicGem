\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the orthogonality viewpoint buys, and what it does not}

Reading the line-sum conditions as $z\perp W$ changes nothing about which arrangements are magic, but it changes which questions are natural, and four of them have been answered above.
First, the test space can be varied: replacing the $2n$ line indicators by the polynomials $x^m,y^m$ ($m\le k$) gives the moment hierarchy, \Cref{prop:hierarchy} locates the degree $k=n-1$ at which polynomial test functions become equivalent to line sums for real heights, and \Cref{thm:perm_threshold} shows that the permutation constraint is not idle: for $n\ge14$ integrality forces equal line sums one degree earlier than linear algebra does.
In this frame the four-term energy $\Elow$ is the degree-one member and is weaker than ``magic'' for every $n\ge4$, while Loly's invariance of the moment of inertia \cite{loly2004invariance} is a consequence of the degree-two member: the value $n^2(n^4-1)/12$ is the constant term of \eqref{eq:loly_identity}, the arrangement-dependent part is the single cross-moment $\Cov(Z,X^2+Y^2)$, and the invariance is weaker than the degree-two level (\Cref{cor:loly}).
Second, $z\perp W$ is a statement about the signed mass $z$: all separable moments vanish (\Cref{thm:linear_balance}), the centre-of-mass offset of an arbitrary arrangement is controlled by $\Efull$ with a sharp constant (\Cref{thm:quant_balance}), and the line-sum conditions say nothing about mixed moments or about unit-vector sums (\Cref{prop:unit_negative}).
Third, each $\Cov(\varphi,Z)$ is a linear permutation statistic, so the energies of a random arrangement have exact moments and a Hoeffding limit law (\Cref{prop:random_energy_moments}, \Cref{cor:random_energy_clt}), and Loly's moment of inertia has an exact null distribution (\Cref{cor:loly_null}); the growth $\E[\Elow]\sim n^4/72$, $\E[\Efull]\sim n^2/6$ is a property of the normalisation $\Cov=\frac1N\sum$, not of the arrangements.
Fourth, because $N^2\Efull$ is a sum of squared integer deviations, a swap changes it by the two-line formula \eqref{eq:swap_general}, which gives local minimality and the exact gap spectrum for all $n$ at once (\Cref{cor:local_min}).

What the viewpoint does not buy is equally clear.
The projection lemma is a restatement of the definition (\Cref{rem:tautology}); it proves nothing about magic squares that the line sums do not already say, and we have not used it to count anything: the counting is done by the meet-in-the-middle enumeration of \Cref{prop:L4}.
The low-mode energy is a poor proxy for the magic property: for $n=4$ its zero set is about $2080$ times larger than the set of magic squares, and even its two-condition sub-classes have $6.7\times10^9$ and $5.0\times10^{10}$ members.

\subsection{Local search on the energy}
\label{sec:algorithms}

By \Cref{thm:swap} a single swap changes $\Efull$ by an explicit quadratic expression in the swapped values, so local search is cheap.
We ran two elementary searches on the symmetric $\Efull$ from uniformly random starting arrangements (\Cref{tab:search}): steepest swap descent (take the swap with the largest decrease; stop when none decreases the energy) and a plain simulated annealing (random swap proposals, Metropolis acceptance, geometric cooling from $T_0$ = the median uphill increment of $N^2\Efull$ at a random arrangement to $T_1=1/2$, over $2\times10^4N$ proposals).
Descent reaches a magic square from $19.5\%$ of random starts for $n=4$ but from only $1.2\%$ ($n=5$) and $0.8\%$ ($n=6$); otherwise it stops after $8$--$14$ swaps on average in a non-magic local minimum with mean residual $N^2\Efull\approx4.4$ (smallest observed residual $1$ for $n=5,6$ and $2$ for $n=4$).
Annealing succeeds in every run for $n=4$ and in $78\%$ ($n=5$) and $50\%$ ($n=6$) of runs within the budget; the failed runs end with a best residual $N^2\Efull$ of $1.05$ ($n=5$) and $1.2$ ($n=6$) on average.
The landscape is thus easy to descend but, as the exhaustive $n=3$ count of non-magic local minima already suggests (eight, all at $\Efull=2/81$), it contains many shallow non-magic minima that a swap move cannot leave.
The experiment is small and the methods elementary; neither competes with the classical constructions or with the backtracking enumerations of \cite{beck2010enumeration,trump2012enumeration}. The energy is a search objective, not a counting tool.

\subsection{Open questions}

\begin{enumerate}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
\item \emph{Size of the low-mode class.} $|L_3|=8$, $|L_4|=14{,}639{,}440$, and the Gaussian local-limit estimate $N!(2\pi)^{-2}\det(\Sigma)^{-1/2}$ is within $20\%$ at $n=4$ and, by Monte Carlo, within about $25\%$ at $n=5,6$ (\Cref{sec:lowmode_n4}). Is $|L_n|/N!\sim c\,n^{-13}$ with an explicit constant, i.e.\ does a local limit theorem hold for the four lattice statistics $(T_1,\dots,T_4)$?
\item \emph{The permutation threshold.} What is $k^*(n)$ for $n\ge14$? The proof of \Cref{thm:perm_threshold} compares the entries of the integer vectors orthogonal to all polynomials of degree $\le k$ with the largest possible deviation $n^2(n-1)/2$. If the smallest such vectors were always alternating binomial vectors supported on $k+2$ consecutive columns, whose largest entry is $\binom{k+1}{\lfloor(k+1)/2\rfloor}\approx2^{k+1}/\sqrt k$, the comparison $2^k\gtrsim n^3$ would give $k^*(n)=O(\log n)$; but shorter integer vectors with small entries might exist, and we have no proof.
\item \emph{Exact unit-vector balance.} Is there a magic square of any order with $B_{\mathrm{ring}}=0$? None exists for $n=3$ and none among the $880$ classes for $n=4$ (smallest value $0.070$); the orthogonality conditions give no handle on this because the radial unit vectors are not in the line space (\Cref{prop:unit_negative}).
\item \emph{Other square families.} The orthogonality formulation and \Cref{sec:theory} apply verbatim to semi-magic, pandiagonal and Franklin squares \cite{loly2007franklin}, with $W$ replaced by the span of the relevant line indicators; Loly's inertia invariance already covers the semi-magic case. The hierarchy, energies and swap formulas for these families have not been worked out.
\end{enumerate}

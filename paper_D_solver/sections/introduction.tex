\section{Introduction: From Static Magic Gems to Interactive Physical Relaxation}
\label{sec:intro}

For more than two millennia---from the $3\times 3$ Lo Shu turtle diagram of ancient China to Albrecht D\"urer's 1514 engraving \emph{Melencolia~I} and Bernard Fr\'enicle de~Bessy's 1693 enumeration of all $880$ order-$4$ squares---magic squares have fascinated mathematicians, artists, and physicists alike \cite{frenicle1693,loubere1693,dudeney1917amusements,andrews1960magic}. A magic square of order $n$ is an arrangement $S : \{1,\dots,n\}^2 \to \{1,\dots,n^2\}$ of the consecutive integers $1\dots n^2$ in an $n\times n$ grid such that every row, every column, and both main diagonals sum to the magic constant $M_n = \frac{1}{2}n(n^2+1)$. When we lift each grid cell into three-dimensional space using centred integer coordinates
\begin{equation}
  X_j = 2j - (n+1), \qquad Y_i = (n+1) - 2i, \qquad Z_{ij} = 2s_{ij} - (n^2+1),
  \label{eq:centred_coords}
\end{equation}
each arrangement $S \in S_{n^2}$ becomes a $3\mathrm{D}$ cloud of $n^2$ points $\mathcal{P}(S) = \{(X_j, Y_i, Z_{ij})\} \subset \Z^3$ whose convex hull $K(S) = \conv\mathcal{P}(S)$ is a polyhedral sculpture that we call a \emph{Magic Gem}. Across the first three papers of this series, we explored the geometry and mechanics of these sculptures once they are already built. In Paper~I \cite{mathewson2026gems}, we classified the polyhedral faces, vertex counts, surface areas, volumes, and symmetry groups of Magic Gems across all $880$ order-$4$ squares and higher-order families. In Paper~II \cite{mathewson2026moments}, we showed that equal row and column sums force the bivariate polynomial moments $\langle X^j, Z\rangle = \langle Y^j, Z\rangle = 0$ to vanish for all $j \ge 0$, which automatically diagonalizes the $3\times 3$ moment-of-inertia tensor of every magic square \cite{loly2004invariance,rogers2004inertia}. In Paper~III \cite{mathewson2026mechanics}, we put those algebraic identities to a physical test, showing that every equal-vertex-mass Magic Gem spins in $3\mathrm{D}$ as an oblate symmetric top ($I_{xx}=I_{yy} < I_{zz}$) immune to intermediate-axis Dzhanibekov tumbling, while solid $3\mathrm{D}$-printed polyhedra remain statically stable on every single one of their $50{,}334$ triangular facets.

Those three studies, however, only examined \emph{completed} magic squares. When demonstrating the $3\mathrm{D}$ Magic Gem viewer to students and colleagues, a natural hands-on question kept coming up: \emph{Instead of typing in a known magic square, what happens if we start with a randomly scrambled grid of numbers $1\dots n^2$, attach virtual physical forces that pull every row, column, and diagonal toward the magic sum $M_n$, and watch the system try to settle into a magic square on its own?} Of course, if our only goal were to write down a single magic square of order $n$ on a computer, we would not need a physical simulation at all: classical closed-form recipes---such as de~La~Loub\`ere's diagonal staircase (Siamese) rule for odd $n$, complementary X-masks for doubly-even $n=4k$, and Conway's LUX method for singly-even $n=4k+2$---construct a solution in $O(n^2)$ steps \cite{loubere1693,andrews1960magic,berlekamp2004winning}, and specialized constraint-based local search (CBLS) algorithms \cite{codognet2001yet,vanhentenryck2005constraint} can assemble magic squares of order $n \sim 100$ by targeting defective lines directly. Our question is different and much closer in spirit to experimental mathematics, statistical physics, and interactive visualization \cite{mezard1987spin,wales2003energy,kirkpatrick1983optimization}: \emph{What does the energy landscape of magic-square constraints actually look like across the $n^2!$ permutations of $S_{n^2}$, why do intuitive physical relaxation schemes get stuck in deceptive non-magic traps, and what cooperative moves unlock them?}

To explore this question visually and interactively, we built an open-source browser laboratory, \texttt{physicalSolver} \cite{mathewson2026physicalsolver}, organized around four interactive web applications: an \emph{Energy Decomposition Explorer} (\texttt{energy-decomposition.html}) that splits any square's error into $3\mathrm{D}$ tilt, line residuals, sliding $k\times k$ sub-window entropies, and complement-pair symmetry breaks; a \emph{Discrete Physics Lab} (\texttt{physics-lab.html}) and \emph{Vector-Physics Explorer} (\texttt{vector-physics.html}) that visualize cell-by-cell stress vectors and test $2$-swap and $3$-cycle descent; and a continuous particle sandbox called the \emph{Bubble Trinity} (\texttt{bubble-trinity.html}), in which the $n^2$ numbers float in a $2\mathrm{D}$ dish as buoyant, size-modulated bubbles driven by line-sum imbalances, core repulsion, and home-cell wells. Playing with these four interactive tools immediately revealed a series of counterintuitive behaviors---such as balanced plates that refuse to become magic, a stubborn $3\times 3$ configuration that traps a quarter of all random shuffles, floating bubbles that lower their field energy on screen yet fail to snap into a valid square, and $6\times 6$ or $10\times 10$ grids that untangle with fewer restarts than $5\times 5$ grids.

Following Whitesides's blueprint-driven approach to connecting interactive experiments with mathematical structure \cite{whitesides2004writing}, this paper provides the complete mathematical and computational explanation for what happens inside \texttt{physicalSolver}. Let $\mathcal{L}_n$ denote the set of $2n+2$ magic lines ($n$ rows, $n$ columns, and $2$ main diagonals), and let $R_L(S) = \sum_{(i,j)\in L} Z_{ij} = 2\bigl(\sum_{(i,j)\in L} s_{ij} - M_n\bigr)$ be the centred residual of line $L \in \mathcal{L}_n$. Stacking the $2n+2$ line indicators as rows of the incidence matrix $A_n \in \{0,1\}^{(2n+2)\times n^2}$, the \emph{line-indicator Hamiltonian} is the sum of squared line residuals:
\begin{equation}
  \Hline(S) = \sum_{L\in\mathcal{L}_n} R_L(S)^2 = \|A_n \mathbf{z}\|_2^2 = \mathbf{z}^\top L_n \mathbf{z}, \qquad L_n = A_n^\top A_n \in \Z^{n^2 \times n^2}.
  \label{eq:hamiltonian_def}
\end{equation}
Because every centred residual $R_L(S)$ is twice the standard line error $\sum_{(i,j)\in L} s_{ij} - M_n$, $\Hline(S)$ is four times the standard sum of squared line errors ($E_{\mathrm{std}} = \frac{1}{4}\Hline$), and $\Hline(S) = 0$ if and only if $S$ is a magic square.

In \Cref{sec:spectral}, we start with the puzzle that opens the \emph{Energy Decomposition Explorer} (\texttt{energy-decomposition.html}): if we adjust a square until its $3\mathrm{D}$ center-of-mass tilt vanishes ($\langle X,Z\rangle = \langle Y,Z\rangle = 0$) and both main diagonals sum to $M_n$ ($R_{D_+} = R_{D_-} = 0$), have we found a magic square? We exhibit a striking $4\times 4$ counterexample $S_{\mathrm{low}}$ (\Cref{fig:spectral}b) that balances flat with zero diagonal error yet has wildly unequal rows and columns ($\Hline = 1{,}584$). Using the Rook's-graph-plus-diagonals decomposition of $L_n = A_n^\top A_n$ \cite{godsil2001algebraic,brouwer2012spectra,loly2009magic}, we show in \Cref{prop:spectral_rationalization} that restricting $L_n$ to the zero-mean hyperplane $\mathbf{1}^\perp$ inhabited by all centred permutations turns its irrational eigenvalues $\lambda_\pm(n)$ into exact integers---$\{0^{[n^2-2n-1]}, (n-2)^{[1]}, n^{[2n-1]}\}$ for even $n$ and $\{0^{[n^2-2n-1]}, (n-1)^{[2]}, n^{[2n-2]}\}$ for odd $n$---with a universal average eigenvalue $\bar{\mu}(\Ltilde_n) = 2$ and random-shuffle expectation $\mathbb{E}[\Hline(S)] = 2\|\mathbf{z}\|^2 = \frac{2}{3}n^2(n^4-1)$ (\Cref{fig:spectral}a). This spectrum immediately reveals why $S_{\mathrm{low}}$ exists (\Cref{rem:low_mode_insufficiency}): linear tilt and diagonal sums only constrain $4$ directions out of the $2n$-dimensional active subspace on $\mathbf{1}^\perp$, leaving $2n-4$ alternating row and column modes completely hidden from rigid-body balance.

In \Cref{sec:windows}, we use the second panel of \texttt{energy-decomposition.html} to peer \emph{inside} the magic ground-state manifold $\Hline(S) = 0$, where $1\mathrm{D}$ line sums can no longer tell one magic square from another. Evaluating sliding $k\times k$ sub-window energies and Shannon entropies $\widetilde{H}_p(k)$ across classical constructions ($n=3,4,5,6,8$) and all $880$ order-$4$ squares (\Cref{fig:window_entropy}), we show in \Cref{lem:quartet_equivalence} that on any even-order magic square ($n=2m$), balancing the $(2m-1)^2$ interior $2\times 2$ quartets automatically balances all boundary-wrapping toroidal quartets, isolating the $48$ panmagic squares of Dudeney Group~I at $n=4$. We then prove the Concentric Shell Quadrupole Identity $M_{xyz}(S) = -8\,Q_{\mathrm{corner}}(S) - 3\,Q_{\mathrm{edge}}(S)$ (\Cref{prop:octupole_census}), which explains both why the complement-break count $B_{\mathrm{comp}}(S) \in \{0,8\}$ is all-or-nothing at order $4$ and why the $3\mathrm{D}$ twist moment $M_{xyz}(S)$ vanishes on the $48$ symmetric associative squares of Group~III plus \emph{exactly $8$ non-symmetric squares} in Groups~IX and~X where the corner and edge shells cancel in a $(-3k, 8k)$ resonance (\Cref{fig:octupole}).

In \Cref{sec:landscape}, we turn to the \emph{Discrete Physics Lab} (\texttt{physics-lab.html}) and \emph{Vector-Physics Explorer} (\texttt{vector-physics.html}), where users watch a scrambled square relax by swapping pairs of cells that lower $\Hline(S)$ according to the quadratic update $\Delta\Hline(\tau_{ab}) = -2d(g_a-g_b) + c_{ab}d^2$ (\Cref{lem:swap_law}). By mapping all $9! = 362{,}880$ permutations of $S_9$ (\Cref{thm:s9_traps} and \Cref{fig:s9_landscape}), we prove that $74.53\%$ of all $3\times 3$ starting grids roll straight down to the Lo Shu magic square, while the remaining $25.47\%$ ($92{,}438$ states) get stuck in a \emph{single} dihedral trap orbit $S_{\mathrm{trap}}^{(3)}$ at $\Hline = 8$. Like a mechanical combination lock, $S_{\mathrm{trap}}^{(3)}$ cannot be improved by any single $2$-swap ($\min\Delta\Hline = +4$) or any $3$-way rotation ($\min\Delta\Hline = +8$). Yet out of all $378$ disjoint $(2,2)$ double swaps on the $3\times 3$ board, \emph{one and only one}---the simultaneous intra-row swap $(6\leftrightarrow 7)(3\leftrightarrow 4)$, acting like a $4$-vertex Kempe chain in graph coloring---unlocks $S_{\mathrm{trap}}^{(3)}$ directly to $\Hline=0$, expanding the deterministic basin of $S_9$ to $100.00\%$.

Finally, in \Cref{sec:bubble}, we analyze the flagship \emph{Bubble Trinity} laboratory (\texttt{bubble-trinity.html}), contrasting continuous $2\mathrm{D}$ floating-bubble relaxation against our discrete \emph{Multi-Exchange Iterated Local Search (ME-ILS)} engine (\Cref{fig:bubble_trinity} and \Cref{tab:physical_analogs_ablation}). Watching the continuous bubbles drift across the four interactive presets (\emph{Frozen Lattice}, \emph{Slow Drift}, \emph{Field Storm}, and \emph{Centrifugal Balance}) reveals a fascinating lesson in continuous vs.\ discrete optimization: the soft Gaussian field energy $\Hind(t)$ drops by $37.5\%$ on screen, yet snapping the bubbles to the grid---whether by greedy nearest-cell assignment or by exact $O(N^3)$ Hungarian bipartite matching---produces $0.0\%$ magic squares, because soft bubbles ``cheat'' by huddling between grid cells to average out positive and negative heights rather than crossing discrete swap barriers. Switching to the discrete ME-ILS hierarchy---which combines $2$-swaps, whole-row/column glides, Kempe-like $(2,2)$ plaquette loops, and threshold-leashed basin restarts---achieves $100.0\%$ synthesis across every order from $n=3$ to $n=10$ (\Cref{tab:multi_order_settler}, up to $100! \approx 9.33\times 10^{157}$ states) and explains why **$5\times 5$ is the hardest frustration bottleneck ($8.03$ restarts)** while larger grids ($n=6\dots 10$, dropping to $3.34$ restarts at $n=10$) become easier to untangle as $f_{\mathrm{null}}(n) = 1 - 2/(n-1)^2$ of the $(2,2)$ plaquette moves become diagonal-free (\Cref{fig:dimensional_relief}).

## General Assessment & Summary of Contributions

This fourth installment in the author's *Magic Gems* series marks a pivot from the static polyhedral and moment-of-inertia properties of completed magic squares (Papers I–III) to the inverse spectral and combinatorial problem: characterizing the quadratic line-sum energy functional
$$\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{line}}(S) = \sum_{L\in\mathcal{L}_n} R_L(S)^2 = \|A_n \mathbf{z}\|_2^2 = \mathbf{z}^\top L_n \mathbf{z}$$
over the symmetric group $S_{n^2}$ of centred height permutations $\mathbf{z}(S) \in \mathbf{1}^\perp \subset \mathbb{Z}^{n^2}$.

From the perspective of algebraic combinatorics and spectral graph theory, the manuscript contains several genuinely striking results that I was not expecting to find in a paper motivated by an interactive browser physics simulator (`physicalSolver`):

1. **Zero-Mean Spectral Rationalization of the Line-Incidence Gram Operator (Proposition 2.1):** For years, those of us who have studied the spectra of magic squares and their underlying line-incidence operators $L_n = A_n^\top A_n$ have regarded the two diagonal constraints as an annoying rank-$2$ perturbation of the Rook's graph $K_n \square K_n$ that introduces awkward quadratic irrationalities $\lambda_\pm(n) = \frac{1}{2}(3n \pm \sqrt{n(n+16)})$ (even $n$) and $\frac{1}{2}(3n+1 \pm \sqrt{n^2+14n+1})$ (odd $n$). Proposition 2.1 demonstrates with admirable economy that because every centred permutation vector $\mathbf{z}(S)$ already lies in the $(n^2-1)$-dimensional hyperplane $\mathbf{1}^\perp$, restricting $L_n$ to $\mathbf{1}^\perp$ projects out the uniform line $\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{1}\}$ and traps the zero-mean projections $\widetilde{\mathbf{d}}_\pm = \mathbf{d}_\pm - \frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}$ entirely inside the $(n-1)^2$-dimensional kernel $\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{row}}\otimes\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{col}}$ of the Rook's operator $L_{\mathrm{rc}}$. The irrational roots $\lambda_\pm(n)$ vanish completely, leaving the exact integer spectrum $\{0^{[n^2-2n-1]}, (n-2)^{[1]}, n^{[2n-1]}\}$ for even $n$ and $\{0^{[n^2-2n-1]}, (n-1)^{[2]}, n^{[2n-2]}\}$ for odd $n$, with universal trace $2(n^2-1)$, mean eigenvalue $\bar{\mu}(\widetilde{L}_n) = 2$, and ensemble expectation $\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\operatorname{Unif}(S_{n^2})}[\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{line}}(S)] = 2\|\mathbf{z}\|^2 = \frac{2}{3}n^2(n^4-1)$. Moreover, Remark 2.2 ("The $4\times 4$ Impostor Square $S_{\mathrm{low}}$") provides a crisp polynomial-moment explanation of why balancing $3\mathrm{D}$ plate tilt and both main diagonals constrains only $4$ modes out of $2n$, leaving $2n-4$ alternating Legendre/Chebyshev-like row and column modes unchecked.
2. **Dudeney Group Classification via Interior/Toroidal $2\times 2$ Quartets and the Concentric Shell Quadrupole Identity (Lemma 3.1 & Proposition 3.2):** Section 3 uses the ground-state manifold $\ker(\widetilde{L}_4) \cap S_{16}$ ($880$ Frénicle squares partitioned into Dudeney's 12 groups) to prove two clean theorems. First, Lemma 3.1 proves that on any even-order magic square $n=2m$, vanishing of the $(2m-1)^2$ interior $2\times 2$ quartet sums is equivalent to vanishing of all $4m^2$ toroidal $2\times 2$ quartet sums, isolating the $48$ panmagic (diabolic) squares of Dudeney Group I at $n=4$. Second—and this is a jewel of a finite-geometry observation—Proposition 3.2 resolves why $56$ rather than $48$ of the $880$ order-$4$ squares have zero mixed octupole moment $M_{xyz}(S) = \sum X_j Y_i Z_{ij} = 0$. By showing that main/anti-diagonal balance enforces $Q_{\mathrm{inner}}(S) = -Q_{\mathrm{corner}}(S)$, the octupole collapses to the Diophantine form $M_{xyz}(S) = -8Q_{\mathrm{corner}}(S) - 3Q_{\mathrm{edge}}(S)$. Beyond the $48$ centrosymmetric (associative) squares of Group III where $Q_{\mathrm{corner}}=Q_{\mathrm{edge}}=0$, exactly $8$ non-associative squares ($B_{\mathrm{comp}}=8$)—four in Group IX (`#200, #208, #390, #711`) and four in Group X (`#492, #494, #794, #811`)—achieve $M_{xyz}=0$ via coprime shell resonance $(Q_{\mathrm{corner}}, Q_{\mathrm{edge}}) = (-3k, 8k)$ at $k \in \{\pm 4, -8\}$, linked by the parity involution $\pi=(2,4,1,3)$ and complementation $S \leftrightarrow 17\mathbf{1}-S$.
3. **Exhaustive $S_9$ Cayley-Graph Landscape and the Bilinear Plaquette Identity up to $n=100$ (Theorems 4.2 & 6.1):** Section 4 exhaustively maps all $9! = 362{,}880$ states of $S_9$ under the $36$ elementary transpositions $\tau_{ab}$, proving that there are exactly $16$ strict local minima: the $8$-element dihedral orbit $D_4 \cdot S_{\mathrm{LoShu}}$ at $\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{line}}=0$ ($74.53\%$ steepest-descent basin) and a single $8$-element dihedral trap orbit $D_4 \cdot S_{\mathrm{trap}}^{(3)}$ at $\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{line}}=8$ ($25.47\%$ basin) that resists all $36$ transpositions and all $168$ directed $3$-cycles, but is unlocked by a unique intra-row $(2,2)$ double swap $(6\leftrightarrow 7)(3\leftrightarrow 4)$ among all $378$ disjoint double transpositions. Sections 5 and 6 then connect the asymptotic fraction $f_{\mathrm{null}}(n) = 1 - 2/(n-1)^2$ of diagonal-free $(2,2)$ plaquette moves to the $n=5$ frustration peak and the $n=6\dots 50$ nullspace-relief plateau, culminating in the Bilinear Plaquette Identity $\Delta\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{line}}(p_1, p_2) = \Delta\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{line}}(p_1) + \Delta\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{line}}(p_2) + 2\,\boldsymbol{\delta R}(p_1)^\top\boldsymbol{\delta R}(p_2)$ (Theorem 6.1), which accelerates exact $(2,2)$ search via rank-$(n+2)$ BLAS multiplication to synthesize $100\times 100$ magic squares in $147.7\text{ s}$ (minimum $34.1\text{ s}$).

The accompanying verification script (`anc/verify_paper_d.py`) is exemplary: every eigenvalue, trace, Dudeney group census, $S_9$ basin count, and $(2,2)$ escape count is verified by exact integer or rational arithmetic (`fractions.Fraction`). Nevertheless, before I can give my unreserved blessing, several matters of algebraic terminology, cycle-space geometry, and subtle discrepancies between `verify_paper_d.py` and the manuscript text must be addressed.

## Detailed Itemized Major Comments

### M1.1. Operator Terminology: Incidence Gram Matrix / Signless Hypergraph Laplacian vs. "Laplacian" $L_n = A_n^\top A_n$
- **Location:** Abstract (line 11), Section 1 (Eq. 2, line 29), Section 2.1 (`sections/spectral_operator.tex`, lines 10–18), Section 7 (line 68), and `anc/verify_paper_d.py` (lines 7, 89).
- **Critique:** In the main text (`sections/spectral_operator.tex`, line 6), the author rightly calls $L_n = A_n^\top A_n$ the "line-incidence operator," and in line 17 notes that $L_{\mathrm{rc}} = A_{\mathrm{Rook}} + 2I_{n^2}$ is the "signless incidence operator of the $n\times n$ Rook's graph $K_n \square K_n$." However, in Section 7 (line 68 of `PAPER_D_READABLE_MANUSCRIPT.md`) and throughout `verify_paper_d.py` (lines 7, 89), $L_n = A_n^\top A_n$ is repeatedly called the *"Rook's-graph-plus-diagonals Laplacian"* or *"Line-Incidence Laplacian."*
- **Mathematical Requirement:** To a spectral graph theorist (cf. Godsil & Royle 2001; Brouwer & Haemers 2012), a standard combinatorial graph Laplacian $L(G) = D(G) - A(G)$ has row sums equal to zero ($L(G)\mathbf{1} = \mathbf{0}$) and has $\mathbf{1}$ in its kernel. Here, $A_n \in \{0,1\}^{(2n+2)\times n^2}$ is the vertex-hyperedge incidence matrix of the $n$-uniform hypergraph $\mathcal{H}_n = ([n]^2, \mathcal{L}_n)$ of magic lines, so $L_n = A_n^\top A_n$ is the **incidence Gram matrix** (or the **signless hypergraph operator**), whose row/column part $L_{\mathrm{rc}} = \sum_i \mathbf{r}_i\mathbf{r}_i^\top + \sum_j \mathbf{c}_j\mathbf{c}_j^\top = A_{\mathrm{Rook}} + 2I_{n^2}$ is related to the true combinatorial Laplacian $\Delta_{\mathrm{Rook}} = 2(n-1)I_{n^2} - A_{\mathrm{Rook}}$ of the Rook's graph by the affine spectral inversion
  $$L_{\mathrm{rc}} = 2n\,I_{n^2} - \Delta_{\mathrm{Rook}}.$$
  That inversion is precisely why the constant vector $\mathbf{1}$ sits at the *top* of the spectrum of $L_{\mathrm{rc}}$ ($\lambda = 2n$) rather than in its kernel ($\lambda = 0$), and why the $(n-1)^2$-dimensional Rook's kernel $\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{row}}\otimes\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{col}}$ corresponds to the highest-eigenvalue eigenspace ($\lambda = 2n$) of $\Delta_{\mathrm{Rook}}$! Please state the identity $L_{\mathrm{rc}} = 2n\,I_{n^2} - \Delta_{\mathrm{Rook}}$ explicitly in Section 2.1 and harmonize the terminology in Section 7 so that $L_n = A_n^\top A_n$ is consistently termed the *line-incidence Gram operator* (or *line-incidence operator*) rather than a combinatorial Laplacian.

### M1.2. Ambient vs. Restricted Operator Dimension for $\widetilde{L}_n|_{\mathbf{1}^\perp}$ in Proposition 2.1
- **Location:** Section 2.1, Proposition 2.1 (`sections/spectral_operator.tex`, lines 36–44; Table 2, line 87) and `anc/verify_paper_d.py` (lines 121–143).
- **Critique:** In Equation (38)–(42) of `sections/spectral_operator.tex` (Proposition 2.1), the author defines $\widetilde{L}_n = P_{\mathbf{1}^\perp} L_n P_{\mathbf{1}^\perp}\big|_{\mathbf{1}^\perp}$ as the restricted operator on the $(n^2-1)$-dimensional subspace $\mathbf{1}^\perp$, and writes its spectrum as:
  $$\operatorname{spec}\bigl(\widetilde{L}_n\big|_{\mathbf{1}^\perp}\bigr) = \begin{cases} \Bigl\{0^{[n^2-2n-1]},\; (n-2)^{[1]},\; n^{[2n-1]}\Bigr\}, & \text{even } n \ge 4, \\ \Bigl\{0^{[n^2-2n-1]},\; (n-1)^{[2]},\; n^{[2n-2]}\Bigr\}, & \text{odd } n \ge 3. \end{cases}$$
  Let us check the sum of the algebraic multiplicities: $(n^2 - 2n - 1) + 1 + (2n - 1) = n^2 - 1 = \dim(\mathbf{1}^\perp)$, which is completely consistent with the $(n^2-1)\times(n^2-1)$ Householder-projected matrix `L_perp = B_perp.T @ L @ B_perp` in `verify_paper_d.py` (line 126), where the extra $0$ eigenvalue along $\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{1}\}$ has been removed by restricting the domain to $\mathbf{1}^\perp$.
- **Mathematical Requirement:** However, in Remark 2.2 (`sections/spectral_operator.tex`, line 101) and Equation (121), the author writes $\widetilde{L}_n$ without the restriction bar $|_{\mathbf{1}^\perp}$. If $\widetilde{L}_n = P_{\mathbf{1}^\perp} L_n P_{\mathbf{1}^\perp}$ is viewed as an $n^2\times n^2$ matrix on $\mathbb{R}^{n^2}$, it has an additional trivial $0$ eigenvalue along $\mathbf{1}$ (multiplicity $n^2 - 2n$ in total), whereas as an endomorphism of $\mathbf{1}^\perp$ its nullity is $\dim\ker(\widetilde{L}_n|_{\mathbf{1}^\perp}) = n^2 - 2n - 1$. Please add a brief clarifying sentence immediately after Equation (36) in `sections/spectral_operator.tex` stating that throughout the paper $\widetilde{L}_n \in \operatorname{End}(\mathbf{1}^\perp)$ denotes the $(n^2-1)\times(n^2-1)$ operator restricted to $\mathbf{1}^\perp$ (so that $\ker(\widetilde{L}_n) = \ker(L_n) \subset \mathbf{1}^\perp$ has dimension $n^2-2n-1$ without the spurious $\mathbf{1}$-mode).

### M1.3. Exact Cycle-Space Interpretation of the $(2,2)$ Plaquette Subspace and the $K_{n,n}$ Bipartite Incidence Graph
- **Location:** Section 1 (Title & Abstract: "Symplectic Plaquette Exchanges"), Section 4.2 (Theorem 4.2, lines 55–63), and Section 5.3 (Equation 8 / lines 161–165 of `PAPER_D_READABLE_MANUSCRIPT.md`).
- **Critique:** In Section 5.3, the author notes that a row- or column-preserving $(2,2)$ plaquette move acts via a vector of the form $(\mathbf{e}_{r_1}-\mathbf{e}_{r_2})\otimes(\mathbf{e}_{c_1}-\mathbf{e}_{c_2})$ inside the $(n-1)^2$-dimensional semi-magic subspace $\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{row}}\otimes\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{col}} = \ker(L_{\mathrm{rc}})$, and in the Title calls these *"Symplectic Plaquette Exchanges."* Yet two crucial algebraic nuances need to be made explicit:
  1. **When is a line-preserving $(2,2)$ double swap actually a $2\times 2$ rectangle $(\mathbf{e}_{r_1}-\mathbf{e}_{r_2})\otimes(\mathbf{e}_{c_1}-\mathbf{e}_{c_2})$?** In Section 4.2 (`sections/swap_landscape.tex`, line 74 and Table 5, line 101; `verify_paper_d.py`, lines 560–575), the neighborhood of line-preserving $(2,2)$ moves has size $2\binom{n}{2}^3$ ($18$ for $n=3$, $432$ for $n=4$), because an intra-row swap in row $r_1$ at columns $(c_1, c_2)$ paired with an intra-row swap in row $r_2$ at columns $(c_3, c_4)$ does **not** require $\{c_1, c_2\} = \{c_3, c_4\}$! Indeed, look at the unique unlocking move for $S_{\mathrm{trap}}^{(3)}$ in Theorem 4.2:
     $$\tau_1 = (6\leftrightarrow 7) \text{ at } (1,2)\leftrightarrow(1,3), \qquad \tau_2 = (3\leftrightarrow 4) \text{ at } (3,1)\leftrightarrow(3,2).$$
     Here Row 1 swaps columns $(2,3)$ while Row 3 swaps columns $(1,2)$! They share column $2$ (which has $R_{c_2}=0$, acting as a pivot column) to transfer residual between column $1$ ($R_{c_1}=-2$) and column $3$ ($R_{c_3}=+2$). Conversely, a strict $2\times 2$ rectangular plaquette $(\mathbf{e}_{r_1}-\mathbf{e}_{r_2})\otimes(\mathbf{e}_{c_1}-\mathbf{e}_{c_2})$ with equal height difference $d_1 = -d_2$ preserves *both* row sums and column sums simultaneously (it is a fundamental $4$-cycle in the cycle space of the complete bipartite graph $K_{n,n}$, whose dimension over $\mathbb{R}$ is precisely $(n-1)^2 = \dim(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{row}}\otimes\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{col}})$!).
  2. **Why "Symplectic"?** The word "Symplectic" appears in the paper's title (*"Symplectic Plaquette Exchanges"*), but nowhere in the body of the manuscript is the word "symplectic" defined or used!
- **Mathematical Requirement:**
  - Either explain in Section 4/5 why these conjugate $(2,2)$ exchanges are termed "symplectic" (e.g., as canonical area/flux-preserving conjugate pair updates in the row–column phase space) **or** replace "Symplectic Plaquette Exchanges" in the title with **"Conjugate Plaquette Exchanges"** or **"Kempe Plaquette Exchanges"** to match the manuscript text.
  - Explicitly distinguish in Section 4.2 and Section 5.3 between: (a) the $\binom{n}{2}^2$ **rectangular $2\times 2$ plaquettes** (fundamental $4$-cycles of $K_{n,n}$ spanning the $(n-1)^2$-dimensional cycle space $\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{row}}\otimes\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{col}} = \ker(L_{\mathrm{rc}})$ when $d_1 = -d_2$), and (b) the broader family of $2\binom{n}{2}^3$ **line-preserving $(2,2)$ double transpositions** (where $\{c_1,c_2\}$ and $\{c_3,c_4\}$ may overlap in a single pivot column, as in Theorem 4.2's unlocking move $(1,2\leftrightarrow 1,3)(3,1\leftrightarrow 3,2)$, so as to alter column sums while preserving row sums). This distinction makes both Theorem 4.2 and the zero-residual relay swap mechanism of Section 6.3 much deeper and clearer.

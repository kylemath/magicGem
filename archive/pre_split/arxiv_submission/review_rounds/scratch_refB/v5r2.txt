                                                 Abstract
          We study Magic Gems: the convex hull of the points obtained by placing the entries of an
     n × n magic square as heights over a centred grid. The representation is D4 -invariant, so each
     essentially different square has one gem. Reading the centred entries as a height function z,
     the magic conditions are exactly the orthogonality of z to a 2n-dimensional space of centred
     line indicators; we treat this as a change of viewpoint, not a theorem, and use it to obtain:
     (i) a moment hierarchy. For real height functions, Cov(X m , Z) = Cov(Y m , Z) = 0 for m ≤ k is
     equivalent to equal row and column sums exactly when k ≥ n − 1 (a Vandermonde fact). For
     permutations of 1, . . . , n2 , integrality lowers the threshold to at most n − 2 for every n ≥ 14.
     Loly’s moment-of-inertia invariance is a consequence of the degree-two level of the hierarchy, and
     the moment of inertia of a uniformly random arrangement has mean equal to Loly’s invariant
     value, with an explicit variance; (ii) exact mean and variance of two energies on a uniformly
     random arrangement, with a limit law by Hoeffding’s combinatorial central limit theorem;
     (iii) an exact single-swap formula, giving strict local minimality and gaps d2 m/n4 ; (iv) the
     exact count 14,639,440 of 4 × 4 arrangements satisfying the four low-mode conditions; (v) an
     exact computation of the geometry of the 880 order-four gems (39 f -vectors, 352 combinatorial
     types, 616 congruence classes, which are exactly the orbits of the complement map S 7→ 17 − S)
     and a computer-assisted proof that Siamese gems have twelve vertices.
     Keywords: magic squares, convex polyhedra, permutation statistics, moment of inertia, com-
     binatorial geometry


1    Introduction
Magic squares are among the oldest objects of recreational mathematics: the 3 × 3 Lo Shu appears
in Chinese sources more than two millennia old, a 4 × 4 square is engraved in Dürer’s Melencolia I
(1514), and systematic constructions for every order have been known since the seventeenth century
[2, 18]. The modern literature treats them from several directions: enumeration and the geometry
of the associated polytopes [21, 8, 1, 4, 3], spectral and matrix properties [15, 16, 17, 5], and, in
work of Loly and collaborators, physical invariants such as the moment of inertia and the multipole
moments [13, 19, 20].

Definition 1.1 (Magic square). An arrangement of order n is a bijection S = (sij ) from the cells
of the n × n grid onto {1, . . . , n2 }. It is a magic square if every row, every column and both main
diagonals sum to the magic constant M (n) = n(n2 + 1)/2, and semi-magic if only the rows and
columns do.

    This paper studies a three-dimensional representation of an arrangement: place the centred
entry sij − (n2 + 1)/2 as a height over the centred cell (j − n−1

questions whose answers are not visible in the line-sum formulation, and the paper is organised
around those answers.

Contributions.

  1. The moment hierarchy (Proposition 4.10, Theorem 4.14). Replacing the line indicators by
     the polynomial test functions xm , y m gives, for each degree k, the conditions Cov(X m , Z) =
     Cov(Y m , Z) = 0 for m ≤ k. For real-valued height functions these are equivalent to equal row
     and column sums if and only if k ≥ n − 1; this is the invertibility of a Vandermonde matrix
     and we state it as a proposition. The result of the section is Theorem 4.14: for permutations
     of 1, . . . , n2 , integrality and the bound on line sums force the threshold k ∗ (n) down to at most
     n − 2 for every n ≥ 14, whereas k ∗ (n) = n − 1 for 3 ≤ n ≤ 13 (computer-verified witnesses,
     Remark 4.15). The “low-mode” energy Elow of earlier versions of this paper is the degree-one
     truncation. Loly’s invariance of the moment of inertia [13], which Loly derived from the
     row and column sums alone and so stated for semi-magic squares, is the single condition
     Cov(Z, X 2 + Y 2 ) = 0: implied by the degree-two conditions, but much weaker than them
     (Corollary 4.12). The same identity gives the null distribution of the moment of inertia of a
     random arrangement (Corollary 4.13).
  2. Exact statistics of a random arrangement (Proposition 5.1). E[Elow ] = (n2 + 1)(n4 − n2 +
     12n − 12)/(72n2 ) and E[Efull ] = (n4 − 1)/(6n2 ), with exact variances; Elow /E[Elow ] → Exp(1)
     by Hoeffding’s combinatorial central limit theorem. Monte Carlo with 108 samples per order
     agrees for n ≤ 8 to within 5 × 10−4 relative error.
  3. The single-swap formula (Theorem 4.17). Exchanging two entries of a magic square that
     differ by d raises Efull by exactly d2 m/n4 , where m ∈ {2, . . . , 6} counts the lines separating
     the two cells; every magic square is a strict local minimum, with minimum gap between 2/n4
     and 6/n4 .
  4. The low-mode class for n = 4 (Proposition 5.4). Exactly 14,639,440 of the 16! arrangements
     satisfy Elow = 0, 7,040 of them magic; the count is a meet-in-the-middle enumeration and
     agrees with a Gaussian local-limit estimate to within 20%.
  5. Geometry of the 880 order-four gems (Section 5.5). 8 to 16 hull vertices, 39 f -vectors,
     352 combinatorial types and 616 congruence classes, which are exactly the orbits of the
     complement map S 7→ 17 − S; no two gems have the same hull-vertex set. The Siamese
     squares of every odd order n ≥ 5 have gems with exactly twelve vertices (Proposition 5.7,
     computer-assisted proof).

What is and is not new. The polyhedral geometry of magic squares is classical: nonnegative
integer matrices with all line sums equal to t are the lattice points of the t-th dilate of the Birkhoff
polytope and their number is a polynomial in t [21, 8]; the count of magic squares with distinct
entries is an Ehrhart quasi-polynomial of an inside-out polytope [4, 3]; the cones of magic squares

2      Background
2.1     Enumeration and symmetry
There are 8 magic squares of order 3, all images of the Lo Shu under the dihedral group D4 of
the square (four rotations, four reflections); there are 7,040 of order 4, forming 880 D4 -orbits first
listed by Frénicle de Bessy (published posthumously in 1693) and verified many times since [3]; and
275,305,224 of order 5, counted by Schroeppel in 1973 and reported by Gardner [9], i.e. 34,413,153
orbits. For n ≥ 6 only statistical estimates are known; see Trump’s compilation [22]. Since D4
maps lines to lines and preserves the entry set, it maps magic squares to magic squares, and two
squares in the same orbit are called essentially different from squares in other orbits. The 880
order-four squares were further sorted by Dudeney into twelve groups according to the pattern of
complementary pairs (a, 17 − a) [7]; we use these groups only as a classical reference partition in
Section 5.5. Classical constructions (Siamese for odd n, diagonal-complement for n ≡ 0 (mod 4),
LUX or Strachey for n ≡ 2 (mod 4)) show that magic squares exist for all n ≥ 3 [2]; we use the
Siamese squares as a family on which hull computations can be pushed to large n.

2.4    Polytopes of magic squares
The polytope literature on magic squares concerns a different polytope from the one studied here.
An n × n nonnegative integer matrix with all row and column sums equal to t is a lattice point
of the t-th dilate of the Birkhoff polytope of doubly stochastic matrices, and their number is a
polynomial in t of degree (n − 1)2 [21, 8, 24]; adding the diagonal conditions and distinct entries
leads to the inside-out polytopes of Beck and Zaslavsky [4], which gave the first enumeration of 4×4
magic squares of arbitrary magic sum [3]; the cones of magic squares and cubes have Hilbert-basis
descriptions [1]; and contingency tables with fixed margins are the same lattice points studied as a
sample space [6]. In all of these the magic squares are the points of a polytope, whereas the Magic
Gem attaches a polyhedron to a single square; the two constructions share nothing beyond the
word. Spectral and matrix properties of a magic square [15, 16, 17], the singular-value signatures
and entropies of Cameron, Rogers and Loly [5], and Loly’s physical invariants—the moment of
inertia [13], the inertia tensor and the multipole moments of a magic cube [19, 20], and the study
of Franklin squares [14]—are closer to our viewpoint: they, too, attach a quantity to one square
and ask what it reveals.

Proposition 3.3 (D4 -invariance). Let g ∈ D4 act on the grid and let gS be the arrangement with
(gS)c = sg−1 c . Then G(gS) = ĝ G(S), where ĝ(x, y, z) = (g(x, y), z) is the isometry of R3 that

congruent, and the gem is an invariant of the D4 -orbit of S.
Proof. In the centred coordinates (3), each g ∈ D4 acts on the cell positions (xij , yij ) as an

the image, so G(gS) = ĝ G(S); ĝ is an isometry because g is orthogonal.

5.6         Siamese gems have twelve vertices
For the Siamese (de la Loubère) squares of odd order the hull-vertex count does not grow with
n. We computed the gems for n = 3, 5, . . . , 21: the Lo Shu gem has f = (8, 16, 10), and every

hull fraction 12/n2 tends to zero. This is a theorem, with a short computer-assisted proof.

Proposition 5.7 (Siamese gems). Let n = 2m + 1 ≥ 5 and let S be the Siamese square of order n
(1 in the middle cell of the top row, successive entries one step up and to the right modulo n, one
step down when blocked). Then G(S) has exactly 12 vertices, 26 edges and 16 facets (12 triangles,
4 quadrilaterals). With 0-indexed cells (i, j) and c = (n2 + 1)/2, the vertices are the points over
the cells
                (0, 0) : c + m + 2,     (0, m − 1) : n2 − 1,   (0, m) : 1,
                (0, 2m) : c + m,        (m − 1, 0) : n2 − m, (m, 0) : m + 2,
                (m, 2m) : n2 − m − 1, (m + 1, 2m) : m + 1, (2m, 0) : c − m,
                (2m, m) : n2 ,          (2m, m + 1) : 2,       (2m, 2m) : c − m − 2,
i.e. the four corners, and on each side of the square the two cells holding the side’s extreme values.
For n = 3 the same construction gives the Lo Shu gem, with 8 vertices (all cells except the centre).

Proof. Step 1: closed form. Write ⟨t⟩ ∈ {0, . . . , n − 1} for t mod n. We claim s(i, j) = n⟨i + j −
m⟩ + ⟨i + 2j − 2m⟩ + 1. Indeed, along a run of n consecutive entries the move (i, j) → (i − 1, j + 1)
fixes i + j and increases i + 2j by 1 modulo n, while the “blocked” move (i, j) → (i + 1, j) increases
both i + j and i + 2j by 1; the entry 1 sits at (0, m) where both expressions vanish. Hence by
induction the entry k lies in a cell with ⟨i + j − m⟩ = ⌊(k − 1)/n⌋ and ⟨i + 2j − 2m⟩ = ⟨k − 1⟩; since
(i, j) 7→ (i + j, i + 2j) is a bijection of Z2n (determinant 1), this determines the cell, which proves
the claim.
      Steps 2 and 3 (sketch; details in Section D). With m = (n − 1)/2, x = j − m, y = m − i, the
closed form gives z = L(x, y)+n ζ(x, y) with L affine and ζ = −n⌊(x−y+m)/n⌋−⌊(2x−y+m)/n⌋ ∈
{±(n+1), ±1, 0}. The affine bijection Φ(x, y, z) = (x, y, (z−L)/n) preserves the face lattice and the
vertex cells, and Φ(G(S)) is the convex hull of the lattice points of five convex lattice polygons at
the five heights ζ: two triangles T , B = −T at ζ = ±(n + 1), two triangles U , D = −U at ζ = ±1,
and a parallelogram M at ζ = 0 (Figure 4a). Sixteen explicit planes, eight of them and their
images under (x, y, ζ) 7→ −(x, y, ζ), are shown to support the convex hull of twelve named points
v1 , . . . , v12 —the three corners of T and of B, one corner each of U and D, and the four corners of
M —in facets that close up into a surface with (V, E, F ) = (12, 26, 16) closed under edge-adjacency,
hence all facets (so P = Q), and every vertex of every layer polygon satisfies all sixteen inequalities
(Figure 4b). These are finitely many polynomial identities and inequalities in m, verified exactly
for all integers m ≥ 2 by the script siamese proof.py. Hence Φ(G(S)) = conv{v1 , . . . , v12 }, and
mapping back by Φ−1 gives the statement; for m = 1 the four triangles degenerate to points and
the same argument gives the 8-vertex Lo Shu gem.

6     Discussion
6.1   What the orthogonality viewpoint buys, and what it does not
Reading the line-sum conditions as z ⊥ W changes nothing about which arrangements are magic,
but it changes which questions are natural, and three of them have been answered above. First,
the test space can be varied: replacing the 2n line indicators by the polynomials xm , y m (m ≤ k)
gives the moment hierarchy, Proposition 4.10 locates the degree k = n − 1 at which polynomial
test functions become equivalent to line sums for real heights, and Theorem 4.14 shows that
the permutation constraint is not idle: for n ≥ 14 integrality forces equal line sums one degree
earlier than linear algebra does. This places two earlier observations in one frame: the four-term
energy Elow is the degree-one member and is weaker than “magic” for every n ≥ 4, while Loly’s
invariance of the moment of inertia [13] is a consequence of the degree-two member—the value
n2 (n4 − 1)/12 is the constant term of (11), the arrangement-dependent part is the single cross-
moment Cov(Z, X 2 + Y 2 ), and the invariance is weaker than the degree-two level (Corollary 4.12).
Second, each Cov(φ, Z) is a linear permutation statistic, so the energies of a random arrangement
have exact moments and a Hoeffding limit law (Proposition 5.1, Corollary 5.2), and Loly’s moment
of inertia has an exact null distribution (Corollary P 4.13); the growth E[Elow ] ∼ n4 /72, E[Efull ] ∼
  2                                                 1
n /6 is a property of the normalisation Cov = N , not of the arrangements. Third, because
N 2 Efull is a sum of squared integer deviations, a swap changes it by the two-line formula (13),
which gives local minimality and the exact gap spectrum for all n at once (Corollary 4.19).
     What the viewpoint does not buy is equally clear. The projection lemma is a restatement of the
definition (Remark 4.8); it proves nothing about magic squares that the line sums do not already
say, and we have not used it to count anything: the counting is done by the meet-in-the-middle
enumeration of Proposition 5.4 and by exact hull computations. The low-mode energy is a poor
proxy for the magic property: for n = 4 its zero set is 2080 times larger than the set of magic
squares, and even its two-condition sub-classes have 6.7 × 109 and 5.0 × 1010 members. The gem
is injective on the 880 order-four classes only up to the complement map (Proposition 5.5), and its
coarse invariants are not: the hull-vertex count takes nine values and the volume 155, and neither is
well aligned with Dudeney’s groups. Finally, the three-dimensional inertia tensor of the unit-mass
gem is uninformative (Remark 4.21); the informative inertia statements are Loly’s planar one and,
for what varies across magic squares, the planar product of inertia.

6.2   Local search on the energy
By Theorem 4.17 a single swap changes Efull by an explicit quadratic expression in the swapped
values, so local search is cheap. We ran two elementary searches on the symmetric Efull from
uniformly random starting arrangements (Table 4): steepest swap descent (take the swap with the
largest decrease; stop when none decreases the energy) and a plain simulated annealing (random
swap proposals, Metropolis acceptance, geometric cooling from T0 = the median uphill increment
of N 2 Efull at a random arrangement to T1 = 1/2, over 2 × 104 N proposals). Descent reaches a
magic square from 19.5% of random starts for n = 4 but from only 1.2% (n = 5) and 0.8% (n = 6);
otherwise it stops after 8–15 swaps in a non-magic local minimum, typically with a single diagonal
off by one (smallest residual N 2 Efull = 1 for n = 5, 6 and 2 for n = 4). Annealing succeeds in every
run for n = 4 and in 78% (n = 5) and 50% (n = 6) of runs within the budget; its failures again end
at N 2 Efull ∈ {1, 2}. The landscape is thus easy to descend but, as the exhaustive n = 3 count of
non-magic local minima already suggests (eight, all at Efull = 2/81), it is riddled with diagonal-only
defects that a swap move cannot repair. The experiment is small and the methods elementary;
neither competes with the classical constructions or with the backtracking enumerations of [3, 22].
The energy is a search objective, not a counting tool.

6.3     Open questions
     1. Size of the low-mode class. |L3 | = 8, |L4 | = 14,639,440, and the Gaussian local-limit es-
        timate N !(2π)−2 det(Σ)−1/2 is within 20% at n = 4 with ratio apparently increasing in n
        (Section 5.4). Is |Ln |/N ! ∼ c n−13 with an explicit constant, i.e. does a local limit theorem
        hold for the four lattice statistics (T1 , . . . , T4 )?
     2. The permutation threshold. What is k ∗ (n) for n ≥ 14? The proof of Theorem 4.14 compares
        the entries of the integer vectors orthogonal to all polynomials of degree ≤ k with the largest
        possible deviation n2 (n − 1)/2. If the smallest such vectors were always alternating binomial
                                                                                                   √

        vectors supported on k +2 consecutive columns, whose largest entry is ⌊(k+1)/2⌋
        the comparison 2k ≳ n3 would give k ∗ (n) = O(log n); but shorter integer vectors with small
        entries might exist, and we have no proof.
     3. Combinatorial types for n = 5. The 34,413,153 order-five classes are within reach of the exact
        hull algorithm; the number of types, the modal f -vector and whether the complement map
        is again the only source of congruence are all computable.
     4. Bounded hull-vertex counts. Siamese gems have twelve vertices for every odd n ≥ 5 because,
        affinely, they are hulls of five lattice polygons whose shapes do not depend on n (Propo-
        sition 5.7). Do the LUX, Strachey or diagonal-complement constructions have the same
        property, and is the hull fraction of a uniformly random magic square of large order bounded
        away from zero?
     5. Other square families. The construction and Section 4 apply verbatim to semi-magic, pandi-
        agonal and Franklin squares [14], with W replaced by the span of the relevant line indicators;
        Loly’s inertia invariance already covers the semi-magic case. The gems of these families have
        not been examined.


7      Conclusion
The Magic Gem is the convex hull of a magic square’s height function over the centred grid, the same
polyhedron for all eight D4 -images of the square. Reading the entries as a height function makes
the magic conditions the orthogonality of that function to a 2n-dimensional space of test functions;
we have treated this as a viewpoint, not a result, and used it to obtain a moment hierarchy whose
degree-two level implies Loly’s inertia invariance and whose permutation threshold drops below
the Vandermonde value n − 1 from n = 14 on; exact moments and a limit law for the energies of a
random arrangement, and the null distribution of Loly’s moment of inertia; an exact single-swap
formula with gap spectrum d2 m/n4 ; the exact count 14,639,440 of 4 × 4 arrangements satisfying
the four low-mode conditions; and an exact computation of the geometry of the 880 order-four
gems, with a proof that Siamese gems have twelve vertices for every odd order. Several statements
of the previous version were artifacts of an asymmetric energy and are withdrawn (Section 1).
What remains is a small set of exact statements about a classical object, and a polyhedron that is
pleasant to look at.

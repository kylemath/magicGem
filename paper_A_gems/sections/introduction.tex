\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Magic squares are among the oldest objects of recreational mathematics: the $3\times3$ Lo Shu belongs to Chinese tradition, a $4\times4$ square is engraved in D\"urer's \emph{Melencolia~I} (1514), and constructions for every order $n\ge3$ have been known for centuries \cite{andrews1960magic,pickover2002zen}.
The modern literature treats them from several directions: enumeration and the geometry of the polytopes whose lattice points are the squares \cite{stanley1973magic,ehrhart1973magic,ahmed2003polyhedral,beck2006insideout,beck2010enumeration}, spectral and matrix properties \cite{loly2009spectra,nordgren2012properties,nordgren2014spectra,cameron2013signatura}, and, in work of Loly and collaborators, physical invariants such as the moment of inertia and the multipole moments \cite{loly2004invariance,rogers2004inertia,rogers2005multipole}.

\begin{definition}[Magic square]
\label{def:magic_square}
An \emph{arrangement} of order $n$ is a bijection $S=(s_{ij})$ from the cells of the $n\times n$ grid onto $\{1,\dots,n^2\}$ (a permutation of $1,\dots,n^2$ written into the grid).
It is a \emph{magic square} if every row, every column and both main diagonals sum to the magic constant $\mu_n=n(n^2+1)/2$, and \emph{semi-magic} if only the rows and columns do.
\end{definition}

\paragraph{The construction.}
Centre the grid at the origin and attach to the cell $(i,j)$ the point
\[
\mathbf p_{ij}=(x_{ij},y_{ij},z_{ij}),
\]
whose horizontal part $(x_{ij},y_{ij})$ is the position of the cell and whose height $z_{ij}=s_{ij}-\frac{n^2+1}{2}$ is the entry minus the mean entry.
Read as vectors from the origin, the $\mathbf p_{ij}$ of a magic square have vertical components that cancel along every row, column and both diagonals (\Cref{lem:lines}; Figures~\ref{fig:vectors} and~\ref{fig:lines}).
The \emph{Magic Gem} $\mathcal G(S)$ is the convex hull of the $n^2$ points (\Cref{sec:gem}).
\Cref{fig:vectors} shows D\"urer's square, its vectors and its gem, which has $12$ vertices and $4$ points in its interior.

\paragraph{Results.}
The gem is the lift of the grid by the height function $z$, so its lower and upper faces are the regular subdivisions of the grid induced by $z$ and $-z$ (\Cref{sec:subdivisions}).
The point set determines the square, but its convex hull keeps only the extreme points.
The results below ask how much of the square the hull still sees.
For every order, the point set has a non-trivial isometry exactly when the square is self-complementary, i.e.\ a rotation or reflection of the grid turns it into its complement (item~1).
At order four the hull has no isometry that the point set lacks, and its vertices alone determine the square (item~2).
For the Siamese squares of every odd order $n\ge5$ the hull sees only the twelve entries that the boundary of the grid forces (item~3).
\begin{enumerate}\setlength\itemsep{2pt}
\item \emph{Symmetry and congruence for every order} (\Cref{thm:pointset}). For a semi-magic square the covariance matrix of the $N=n^2$ points is $\operatorname{diag}(a_n,a_n,b_n)$ with $a_n=(n^2-1)/12\ne b_n=(n^4-1)/12$. Hence every isometry of the point set lies in $D_4\times\{\pm1\}$, the point set has a non-trivial isometry if and only if $S$ is self-complementary, and two semi-magic squares have congruent point sets if and only if one is a $D_4$-image of the other or of its complement.
\item \emph{The gems of order four} (\Cref{thm:symmetry}, \Cref{sec:census}). There are $880$ magic squares of order four up to rotation and reflection ($D_4$-classes). The isometry group of each gem equals that of its point set; so $48$ gems (the associative squares) are centrally symmetric, $304$ have one half-turn, and $528$ have no symmetry. We tabulate the exact geometry ($8$ to $16$ vertices, $39$ $f$-vectors, $352$ combinatorial types, $155$ distinct volumes, $616$ congruence classes) and show that the vertex set alone determines the square up to $D_4$ (\Cref{prop:gem-injective}).
\item \emph{Siamese gems} (\Cref{thm:siamese}). For the Siamese square of every odd order $n\ge5$ the vertices of the gem are exactly the twelve vertices that the boundary of the grid forces (\Cref{lem:forced}), the $f$-vector is $(12,26,16)$, and the isometry group is $\{\pm\mathrm{id}\}$. The proof is complete on paper: a three-case reduction, a table of $128$ polynomial slacks (a slack is the right-hand side minus the left-hand side of a facet inequality, evaluated at a point), and a closing argument on $16$ facets. Other linear magic squares can have many more vertices: up to $18$, $25$, $31$ and $36$ for $n=5,7,9,11$, and as few as $11$ at order five, while some order-five magic squares that are not linear have only $10$ (\Cref{rem:linear}).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Relation to previous work.}
The polyhedral geometry of magic squares is classical: nonnegative integer matrices with all line sums equal to $t$ are the lattice points of the $t$-th dilate of the Birkhoff polytope, and their number is a polynomial in $t$ \cite{stanley1973magic,ehrhart1973magic,ziegler1995lectures}; the count of magic squares with distinct entries is an Ehrhart quasi-polynomial of an inside-out polytope \cite{beck2006insideout,beck2010enumeration}; the cones of magic squares and cubes have Hilbert-basis descriptions \cite{ahmed2003polyhedral}; contingency tables with fixed margins are the same lattice points seen as a sample space \cite{diaconis1995rectangular}.
In all of these the magic squares are the \emph{points} of a polytope, whereas the Magic Gem attaches a polytope to a \emph{single} square.
The lifting construction itself is that of regular subdivisions and secondary fans \cite{gelfand1994discriminants,deloera2010triangulations}.
The moment identities of \Cref{sec:moments} are due in substance to Loly and Rogers \cite{loly2004invariance,rogers2004inertia,rogers2005multipole}; we record them in the coordinates of the gem for later use.
The partition of the $880$ order-four squares into associative, self-complementary and other squares is Dudeney's \cite{dudeney1917amusements}.
New are the Siamese theorem and the exact order-four census, together with a short covariance argument that settles symmetry and congruence of the point sets for every order.
The reading of the line conditions as orthogonality conditions on the centred entries and the statistics of random arrangements are treated in a companion manuscript \cite{mathewson_companion}; no result of it is used here.

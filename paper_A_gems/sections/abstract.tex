\begin{abstract}
What does a magic square look like as a three-dimensional polyhedron? Given an arrangement of $\{1,\dots,n^2\}$ in an $n\times n$ grid, we lift each cell to $\R^3$ by centring its grid coordinates $(x,y)$ at the origin and taking its centred entry $z = s_{ij} - (n^2+1)/2$ as a vertical height. We call the convex hull of these $n^2$ points the \emph{gem} of the arrangement; its lower and upper boundaries are the regular polyhedral subdivisions of the grid induced by the height field $\pm z$.

While random arrangements and generic linear magic squares produce gems whose vertex counts grow with $n$, our main theorem shows that the classical Siamese (de la Loub\`ere) magic square of every odd order $n\ge5$ behaves at the opposite extreme: its gem has the same combinatorial type for all $n\ge5$, with $f$-vector $(12,26,16)$, isometry group $\{\pm\mathrm{id}\}$, and only the twelve vertices forced by the four corners and the extreme entries along the four sides of the grid. The proof shears the lifted point set onto five parallel planes and reduces the convex hull calculation to the signs of a finite table of polynomials in $m=(n-1)/2$. The same shearing method yields the constant $f$-vector $(12,24,14)$ for the base-$n$ digit-exchanged square, and we determine and count all magic linear squares. Along every family of linear squares with fixed steps and offsets affine in $n$ the number of vertices is bounded and the $f$-vector is eventually periodic in $n$, whereas over all linear magic squares of prime order $n$ the maximum number of vertices grows as $\Theta(\ln n)$.

At general order, we show that several foundational properties of a magic gem require weaker hypotheses than the full magic line sums. Whenever the height field has vanishing first moments—as holds for every semi-magic square—a short principal-axes argument proves that the lifted point set has a non-trivial Euclidean symmetry if and only if the arrangement equals its complement up to dihedral symmetry. An exact census of the $880$ magic squares of order four yields $616$ congruence classes and $352$ combinatorial types of gems, none having a hull isometry absent from its point set. Remarkably, the combinatorial invariants of a gem (such as vertex and facet counts) are nearly insensitive to the magic line-sum constraints, whereas continuous geometric invariants—most notably the volume and the solid centroid—shift systematically because zero-sum lines prevent the height field from curving uniformly upward or downward.

\end{abstract}

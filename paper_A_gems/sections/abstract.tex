\begin{abstract}
To an $n\times n$ magic square $S$ we attach the points $\mathbf p_{ij}=(x_{ij},y_{ij},z_{ij})\in\R^3$, where $(x_{ij},y_{ij})$ is the centred cell and $z_{ij}$ the centred entry, and call their convex hull the \emph{Magic Gem} $\mathcal G(S)$; its lower and upper faces project onto the regular subdivisions of the $n\times n$ grid induced by the heights $z$ and $-z$.
For every semi-magic square of every order the covariance matrix of the point set is $\operatorname{diag}(a_n,a_n,b_n)$ with $a_n\neq b_n$; we deduce that the point set has a non-trivial isometry exactly when the square is self-complementary, and that the point sets of two semi-magic squares are congruent exactly when the squares agree up to the dihedral group and complementation.
For the $880$ magic squares of order four we compute the gems exactly and show that no gem has an isometry beyond those of its point set: $48$ gems are centrally symmetric, $304$ have a single half-turn, the other $528$ are asymmetric and chiral, and the gems fall into $616$ congruence classes, $352$ combinatorial types and $39$ $f$-vectors, with $8$ to $16$ vertices.
For the Siamese (de la Loub\`ere) square of every odd order $n\ge5$ we prove that the gem has no vertices other than the twelve that the boundary of the grid forces (the four corners and, on each side, the cells of the smallest and largest entry), that the gem has $f$-vector $(12,26,16)$, and that its isometry group is $\{\pm\mathrm{id}\}$; the proof reduces to a table of slack polynomials in $m=(n-1)/2$.
Over all linear magic squares of orders $5$, $7$, $9$, $11$ the largest vertex count is $18$, $25$, $31$, $36$, so bounded vertex counts are not a feature of linear constructions in general.

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} magic squares, convex polytopes, regular subdivisions, symmetry groups, Siamese method

\end{abstract}

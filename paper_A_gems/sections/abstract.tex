\begin{abstract}
To an arrangement of $1,\dots,n^2$ in an $n\times n$ grid we attach the $n^2$ points $(x,y,z)$ in $\R^3$, where $(x,y)$ is the position of a cell measured from the centre of the grid and $z$ is its entry minus the mean entry.
Their convex hull is the \emph{gem} of the arrangement; its lower and upper faces are the regular subdivisions of the grid induced by the heights and by their negatives.
Magic squares are the motivating case.
Our main result concerns the Siamese (de la Loub\`ere) square of every odd order $n\ge5$: its gem has exactly the twelve vertices that the boundary of the grid forces (the corners and, on each side, the cells of the smallest and largest entry), with $f$-vector $(12,26,16)$ and isometry group $\{\pm\mathrm{id}\}$.
The proof shears the lift onto five levels and reduces to the signs of a table of polynomials in $m=(n-1)/2$.
The same method gives the $f$-vector $(12,24,14)$ for the square with the two base-$n$ digits exchanged, and we determine and count the magic linear squares.
Along every family of linear squares with fixed steps and offsets affine in $n$ the number of vertices is bounded and the $f$-vector is eventually periodic in $n$, while over all linear magic squares of prime order $n$ the largest number of vertices is of order $\Theta(\ln n)$.

Most other results need less than magic.
If the heights have vanishing first moments, as for every semi-magic square, the point set has a non-trivial isometry exactly when the arrangement equals its complement up to rotation and reflection.
For the $880$ magic squares of order four a computational census gives $616$ congruence classes and $352$ combinatorial types of gems, none with an isometry that its point set lacks.
The gem hardly sees the magic conditions: at order four the mean numbers of vertices and of lower and upper facets of magic gems differ from those of semi-magic squares and of random arrangements by at most $0.14$ standard deviations, and the excess of vertices over semi-magic squares comes almost entirely from self-complementary squares; at order five, on samples, the mean vertex counts differ by less than $0.07$ vertices.
The volume and the solid centroid do shift.

\end{abstract}

\section{Faces of the gem and a census of order four}
\label{sec:census}

After two facts about the faces of every gem, this section reports a computational census of the gems of the $880$ magic squares of order four (vertices, faces, volumes, combinatorial types, congruence classes) and compares them with the gems of other arrangements.

\subsection{Forced vertices and regular subdivisions}
\label{sec:forced}

The \emph{sides} of the grid are its first and last rows and columns.
Every gem lies in the box $\mathcal B_n=[-\frac{n-1}2,\frac{n-1}2]^2\times[-\frac{N-1}2,\frac{N-1}2]$ and meets each of its six faces: the top and the bottom face in the points of the entries $N$ and $1$, and the vertical face over a side $e$ of the grid in the polygon $\operatorname{conv}\{\mathbf p_c: c\in e\}$.
Each contact is a face of $\mathcal G(S)$, so its vertices are vertices of $\mathcal G(S)$; the lemma below names those that can be read off from the entries.

\begin{lemma}[Forced vertices]
\label{lem:forced}
Let $S$ be an arrangement of order $n\ge2$. The following points are vertices of $\mathcal G(S)$; we call them the \emph{forced vertices} of $S$: the points over the four corner cells; the points carrying the entries $1$ and $N$; and, for each side, the points over the cells of the smallest and of the largest entry on that side.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
A point that is the unique maximiser of a linear functional over the $N$ points is a vertex.
The entries are distinct, so $\mp z$ have unique maximisers where $s=1$ and $s=N$.
The functional $-x+y$ attains its maximum only at the top-left corner, and similarly for the other corners.
On the left column $x=-\frac{n-1}2$, while $x\ge-\frac{n-1}2+1$ elsewhere; for $\varepsilon=1/N$ the functional $-x+\varepsilon z$ exceeds $\frac{n-1}2-\frac12$ on the left column and is less than $\frac{n-1}2-\frac12$ elsewhere, so its unique maximiser is the point of largest height on the left column; $-x-\varepsilon z$ gives the smallest, and the other sides follow by symmetry.
\end{proof}

For order four the forced vertices number between $4$ and $13$, and in general other points are vertices as well (\Cref{sec:census-4}).

\paragraph{Gems and regular subdivisions.}
\label{sec:subdivisions}
Geometrically, a three-dimensional gem $\mathcal G(S)$ is bounded over the square $\operatorname{conv}A=[-\frac{n-1}2,\frac{n-1}2]^2$ by a piecewise-linear concave upper roof $z=U(x,y)$ and a piecewise-linear convex lower floor $z=L(x,y)$, joined around the perimeter of the grid by vertical walls wherever the upper and lower boundary chains do not coincide.
Viewing the gem from below ($z\to-\infty$) or from above ($z\to+\infty$) projects its lower and upper facets onto two \emph{regular subdivisions} of the $n\times n$ grid $A$ of cell positions (\Cref{fig:construction}e shows both subdivisions for the Lo Shu).
More precisely, for any height function $\omega\colon A\to\R$, the regular subdivision $\mathcal S(\omega)$ of $A$ is obtained by projecting to the plane the lower faces of $\operatorname{conv}\{(c,\omega(c)):c\in A\}$, i.e.\ the faces whose outer normals have negative vertical coordinate; its cells are the subsets of grid points whose lifts lie on a common lower face \cite[Ch.~2]{deloera2010triangulations}, \cite[Ch.~7]{gelfand1994discriminants}.
(Each cell of $\mathcal S(\omega)$ is recorded as a marked subset of $A$, since the convex hull of a cell may contain grid points whose lifted heights lie strictly above the supporting plane and therefore do not belong to the cell.)
The subdivision $\mathcal S(\omega)$ is unchanged if $\omega$ is multiplied by a positive scalar or if an affine function of $(x,y)$ is added to it; the height functions that induce a given subdivision form a relatively open polyhedral cone, its \emph{secondary cone}.
Because the upper faces of $\mathcal G(S)$ are the reflections in $z=0$ of the lower faces of the lift by $-z$, the lower and upper boundaries of $\mathcal G(S)$ project to $\mathcal S(z)$ and $\mathcal S(-z)$.

\begin{proposition}[Gems as pairs of regular subdivisions]
\label{prop:subdivisions}
Let $S$ be an arrangement of order $n\ge2$ that is not a $D_4$-image of the natural square.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Every facet of $\mathcal G(S)$ is a lower facet, an upper facet or a vertical facet. Projection to the plane maps the lower facets onto the maximal cells of $\mathcal S(z)$ and the upper facets onto the maximal cells of $\mathcal S(-z)$.
\item The vertical facets lie in the four planes $x=\pm\frac{n-1}2$, $y=\pm\frac{n-1}2$; the plane over a side of the grid contains a facet if and only if the entries along that side, in cell order, are not an arithmetic progression.
\item $\mathbf p_c$ is a vertex of $\mathcal G(S)$ if and only if $c$ is a vertex of a cell of $\mathcal S(z)$ or of $\mathcal S(-z)$.
\item The face lattice of $\mathcal G(S)$, with vertices labelled by cells, is determined by the pair $(\mathcal S(z),\mathcal S(-z))$, hence by the secondary cones containing $z$ and $-z$. The complement $N+1-S$ exchanges the two subdivisions.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(1) follows directly from the definition of $\mathcal S(z)$ and, after reflecting in $z=0$, of $\mathcal S(-z)$.
For (2), a vertical facet lies in a supporting plane $\ell(x,y)=\max_A\ell$ for some non-zero linear form $\ell$; the maximisers of $\ell$ on the grid $A$ are either a single corner or the $n$ cells of a side, and the resulting face is two-dimensional if and only if we are in the second case and the lifted points over that side are not collinear—that is, the heights along the side are not an affine function of the position.
For (3), the vertical line through any vertex $\mathbf p_c$ meets $\mathcal G(S)$ in a segment having $\mathbf p_c$ as an endpoint, so $\mathbf p_c$ lies on a lower or an upper face, of which it is a vertex; conversely, the vertices of any face are vertices of the polytope.
For (4), consider the upper concave roof $U$ and lower convex floor $L$ of $\mathcal G(S)$ over $\operatorname{conv}A$.
The vertical thickness $U-L$ is concave, non-negative, and strictly positive at the projection of the interior point $\mathbf 0$ (\Cref{prop:fulldim}), so $U>L$ throughout $\operatorname{int}\operatorname{conv}A$; thus a lower face and an upper face can meet only over the boundary $\partial\operatorname{conv}A$.
Over each side $e$ of the grid, $\mathcal G(S)$ meets the vertical boundary plane in the polygon $\operatorname{conv}\{\mathbf p_c: c\in e\}$, which is bounded above and below by the restrictions of $U$ and $L$ to $e$; this polygon is a two-dimensional vertical facet if and only if those two boundary chains do not coincide into a single segment, i.e.\ by (2) when the entries along $e$ are not an arithmetic progression.
Finally, each lower or upper facet projects bijectively onto its cell in $A$, so its vertices are the lifts of the extreme points of that cell.
Hence the marked cells of $\mathcal S(z)$ and $\mathcal S(-z)$, together with the vertical facets, determine all vertex--facet incidences, and replacing $S$ by its complement $N+1-S$ negates $z$ and swaps $\mathcal S(z)$ with $\mathcal S(-z)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Lo Shu, continuing \Cref{ex:loshu}]
\label{ex:loshu-subdiv}
Writing each grid point as its entry, $\mathcal S(z)$ has the maximal cells $\{1,2,6\}$, $\{1,3,8\}$, $\{1,2,3,4\}$, and $\mathcal S(-z)$ has $\{2,7,9\}$, $\{4,8,9\}$, $\{6,7,8,9\}$.
The vertical facets are the triangles $\{2,4,9\}$, $\{2,6,7\}$, $\{1,6,8\}$, $\{3,4,8\}$.
The centre point (height $0$) lies in the convex hull of each quadrilateral cell but belongs to no cell: the plane of $\{1,2,3,4\}$ is $z=-x-y-3$, which is $-3<0$ at the centre.
\end{example}

For the $880$ order-four gems, $856$ have four vertical facets, $20$ have three and $4$ have two; the height functions of the $7040$ order-four magic squares induce $417$ distinct regular subdivisions of the $4\times4$ grid up to $D_4$, and for $548$ of the $880$ classes $\mathcal S(z)$ is a triangulation.

\subsection{The census}
\label{sec:census-4}

There are $8$ magic squares of order $3$, all images of the Lo Shu under $D_4$, and $7{,}040$ of order $4$, forming $880$ $D_4$-classes; the order-four squares were listed by Fr\'enicle de Bessy \cite{frenicle1693} and have been recounted by computer \cite{beck2010enumeration}.
Dudeney sorted them into twelve groups according to the pattern of complementary pairs $(a,17-a)$ \cite{dudeney1917amusements} (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix} records the group labels for all $7040$ squares).
The number $275{,}305{,}224$ of magic squares of order five up to $D_4$ is due to Schroeppel (1973) \cite{gardner1976}; for order six see \cite{pinn1998number,mino2024order6,trump_howmany}.

For each of the $880$ squares we computed $\mathcal G(S)$ exactly (integer facet inequalities, rational volumes; Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix}).

\paragraph{Vertices, faces and volumes.}
Between $8$ and all $16$ points are vertices; $V=12$ is the most frequent value ($380$ gems), and the mean is $269/22\approx12.23$.
The $39$ $f$-vectors range from $(8,14,8)$ (one gem) to $(16,36,22)$ (nine gems); the most frequent is $(12,26,16)$ ($164$ gems).
The volume takes $155$ distinct values, from $58$ to $292/3$, with mean $84.05$; its denominators are $1,2,3,6$, because for even $n$ all coordinates are half-odd integers, so that $\mathcal G(S)+(\frac12,\frac12,\frac12)$ is a lattice polytope.
The Ehrhart polynomials of these lattice polytopes take $554$ distinct values (Appendix~\ref{sec:ehrhart}).

\paragraph{Combinatorial types.}
Two gems have the same combinatorial type if their vertex--facet incidence graphs are isomorphic by an isomorphism that maps vertices to vertices and facets to facets.
Testing pairs of incidence graphs for isomorphism gives $352$ types (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix}).
By \eqref{eq:complement} the gems of $S$ and $\overline S$ are mirror images, so every type is a union of complement orbits; the $93$ types with one member are self-complementary squares, and of the $201$ types with two members, $177$ are mirror pairs and $24$ consist of two self-complementary squares.
The numbers of types with $1,2,3,\dots$ members are
\[
\begin{array}{r|rrrrrrrrrrrrrr}
\text{squares per type} & 1&2&3&4&5&6&7&8&10&13&14&16&26&32\\\hline
\text{types} & 93&201&8&20&4&7&4&5&5&1&1&1&1&1
\end{array}
\]
The largest type, with $f=(10,22,14)$ (twelve triangles and two quadrilaterals), has $32$ members.

\begin{proposition}[Congruence classes of the order-four gems; exact computation]
\label{prop:congruence}
Let $S$ and $T$ be magic squares of order four. Then $\mathcal G(S)$ and $\mathcal G(T)$ are congruent if and only if $T\in D_4S\cup D_4\overline S$. There are $616$ congruence classes of gems: $352$ self-complementary classes and $264$ mirror pairs.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
``If'' is \eqref{eq:d4} and \eqref{eq:complement}. For ``only if'': the sorted multiset of pairwise squared distances of the vertex set is a congruence invariant, and it takes $616$ distinct values over the $880$ gems (exact computation). Gems in one complement orbit are congruent, and there are $616$ orbits ($352$ self-complementary classes and $264$ pairs). Hence the congruence classes are exactly the complement orbits.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[The vertex set determines the square]
\label{rem:vertexset}
Remarkably, the vertices of an order-four magic gem suffice to reconstruct the entire magic square: if $S$ and $T$ are magic squares of order four and $\hat g\operatorname{vert}\mathcal G(S)=\operatorname{vert}\mathcal G(T)$ for some $g\in D_4$, then $T=gS$.
To see why, note that by \eqref{eq:d4}, $gS$ and $T$ share the same vertex set, so they agree on all vertex cells and leave the same subset $U$ of non-vertex cells undetermined.
The ten magic line-sum conditions provide a system of linear equations for the entries on $U$, governed by the $0/1$ incidence matrix $A_U$ between the ten lines and the cells of $U$. Whenever $\operatorname{rank}A_U=|U|$, the missing entries are uniquely determined by linear algebra alone.
Direct calculation shows that $\operatorname{rank}A_U=|U|$ for $871$ of the $880$ $D_4$-classes.
For the remaining nine classes ($\#45$, $\#46$, $\#47$, $\#49$, $\#50$, $\#54$, $\#165$, $\#166$ and $\#672$), enumerating all order-four magic squares that agree with $S$ on its vertex cells reveals that five classes admit no other completion, while $\#45$, $\#47$, $\#50$ and $\#54$ admit $1$, $1$, $7$ and $7$ alternative magic completions respectively (for instance, $\#109$ agrees with $\#45$ on its twelve vertices). In every such case, however, the alternative completion turns at least one cell of $U$ into a new vertex of the hull, so no two distinct squares share the exact same vertex set.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Forced vertices and the geometry of the extreme squares.}
For order four nothing beyond the forced vertices of \Cref{lem:forced} is a vertex of every gem: the only cells that carry a vertex in all $880$ gems are the four corners, and the only entries that always label a vertex are $1$ and $16$.
In eight gems the point of the entry $2$ is a non-vertex boundary point, for instance for square \#593 below.
The gems of the Lo Shu, of D\"urer's square and of the Siamese square of order $5$ have $8$ of $9$, $12$ of $16$ and $12$ of $25$ points as vertices and volumes $18$, $92$ and $830/3$.

The three extreme order-four gems highlighted in \Cref{fig:gem-geometry-880}(d)--(f) illustrate how the placement of the entries governs the polyhedral geometry:
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
\item \emph{Minimum vertices and facets ($\#54$, $f=(8,14,8)$):} In square $\#54$ below, the top row $(1,6,11,16)$ and the bottom row $(4,7,10,13)$ are both arithmetic progressions. By \Cref{prop:subdivisions}(2), the lifted points along each of these two sides of the grid are collinear, eliminating both the front and back vertical facets and making $\#54$ one of the four order-four gems with only two vertical facets and the unique gem achieving the minimal $f$-vector $(8,14,8)$.
\item \emph{All sixteen points as vertices ($\#802$, $V=16$):} The gem of D\"urer's square has $12$ boundary vertices and its $4$ central cells in the interior. Exchanging its first two rows and its last two rows produces square $\#802$, which moves the extreme entries $\{1,2,15,16\}$ from the boundary into the interior $2\times2$ core so that all four interior points pierce through the boundary canopy and all $16$ points become vertices.
\item \emph{Maximum volume ($\#379$, $\operatorname{vol}=292/3$):} In square $\#379$, the four smallest entries $\{1,2,3,4\}$ and four largest entries $\{13,14,15,16\}$ alternate along the boundary and diagonals so that the upper concave surface $U$ is propped near the ceiling across the grid while the lower convex surface $L$ is pulled near the floor, maximizing the enclosed volume.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Which invariants see the line conditions}
\label{sec:magic-vs-random}

To understand how the line-sum conditions shape the geometry of the gem, we compare four nested families of arrangements of order four (\Cref{tab:magic-vs-random}): all $16!\approx2.09\cdot10^{13}$ arrangements, the $6{,}697{,}177{,}424$ arrangements whose heights have vanishing first moments $\sum z\,x=\sum z\,y=0$ (about one in $3124$), the $549{,}504$ semi-magic squares, and the $7040$ magic squares.

As \Cref{tab:magic-vs-random} shows, the combinatorial counts are almost completely insensitive to the line-sum constraints.
The mean number of vertices rises only slightly from $12.06$ for random arrangements and $12.12$ for semi-magic squares to $12.23$ for magic squares, while the mean numbers of lower and upper facets differ by less than $0.08$ across all four families and the mean number of vertical facets stays between $3.97$ and $4.00$.
Even the small difference in vertex count between magic and semi-magic squares is largely an artifact of symmetry: self-complementary magic squares have more vertices on average ($12.43$ versus $12.09$ for asymmetric magic squares, with the $48$ associative squares averaging $11.88$), and self-complementary squares constitute $40\%$ of the magic squares compared with $10\%$ of the semi-magic squares. Restricting to non-self-complementary squares reduces the gap between magic and semi-magic mean vertex counts to $0.017\pm0.004$.

By contrast, the continuous geometric invariants respond noticeably as each successive balance condition is imposed:
\begin{enumerate}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
\item \emph{Vanishing first moments} align the principal axes of the solid with the coordinate axes (reducing the off-diagonal tilt $(\Sigma_{xz}^2+\Sigma_{yz}^2)^{1/2}$ of the solid's second-moment matrix from $0.73$ to $0.24$) and increase the mean volume from $75.75$ to $81.72$, without yet pulling the solid centroid toward the origin.
\item \emph{Equal row and column sums} (the semi-magic condition) draw the solid centroid more than halfway toward the origin (from distance $0.35$ to $0.16$) and raise the mean volume to $82.68$.
\item \emph{Equal main diagonal sums} increase the mean volume further to $84.05$ and pull the mean centroid distance down to $0.11$, an effect that persists even when self-complementary squares are excluded.
\end{enumerate}
There is a clean geometric reason why zero-sum lines inflate the volume of the hull. By \Cref{prop:subdivisions}(4), $\operatorname{vol}\mathcal G(S)=\iint(U-L)$, where $U$ is the concave upper boundary function and $L$ is the convex lower boundary function of the gem. Along any line $L_0$ of the grid on which $\sum_{c\in L_0}z_c=0$, the inequalities $L(c)\le z_c\le U(c)$ force
\[
\sum_{c\in L_0}L(c)\;\le\;0\;\le\;\sum_{c\in L_0}U(c).
\]
Consequently, $z$ cannot be uniformly convex or uniformly concave along $L_0$: a bowl-shaped or dome-shaped height profile (which would make $U$ and $L$ hug each other closely and produce a thin, small-volume hull) is forbidden along every zero-sum line, forcing the upper canopy $U$ and lower bowl $L$ apart.

A similar pattern appears in heuristic explorations at order five. Comparing $2\cdot10^4$ certified exact hulls each of uniform random arrangements, random arrangements with vanishing first moments, and order-five semi-magic and magic squares generated by simulated annealing (Appendix~\ref{sec:vertexstats}; note that the sampling distribution of the annealer at $n=5$ is not known to be uniform), the mean vertex counts are nearly identical across all four families ($15.40$, $15.42$, $15.36$ and $15.39$, with standard errors $0.012$), whereas the mean volumes climb steadily ($250.1$, $260.9$, $265.4$ and $268.5$) and the mean distance of the solid centroid from the origin drops from $0.47$ to $0.24$ and $0.22$.

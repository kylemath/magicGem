\section{Faces of the gem and a census of order four}
\label{sec:census}

After two facts about the faces of every gem, this section reports a computational census of the gems of the $880$ magic squares of order four (vertices, faces, volumes, combinatorial types, congruence classes) and compares them with the gems of other arrangements.

\subsection{Forced vertices and regular subdivisions}
\label{sec:forced}

The \emph{sides} of the grid are its first and last rows and columns.
Every gem lies in the box $\mathcal B_n=[-\frac{n-1}2,\frac{n-1}2]^2\times[-\frac{N-1}2,\frac{N-1}2]$ and meets each of its six faces: the top and the bottom face in the points of the entries $N$ and $1$, and the vertical face over a side $e$ of the grid in the polygon $\operatorname{conv}\{\mathbf p_c: c\in e\}$.
Each contact is a face of $\mathcal G(S)$, so its vertices are vertices of $\mathcal G(S)$; the lemma below names those that can be read off from the entries.

\begin{lemma}[Forced vertices]
\label{lem:forced}
Let $S$ be an arrangement of order $n\ge2$. The following points are vertices of $\mathcal G(S)$; we call them the \emph{forced vertices} of $S$: the points over the four corner cells; the points carrying the entries $1$ and $N$; and, for each side, the points over the cells of the smallest and of the largest entry on that side.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
A point that is the unique maximiser of a linear functional over the $N$ points is a vertex.
The entries are distinct, so $\mp z$ have unique maximisers where $s=1$ and $s=N$.
The functional $-x+y$ attains its maximum only at the top-left corner, and similarly for the other corners.
On the left column $x=-\frac{n-1}2$, while $x\ge-\frac{n-1}2+1$ elsewhere; for $\varepsilon=1/N$ the functional $-x+\varepsilon z$ exceeds $\frac{n-1}2-\frac12$ on the left column and is less than $\frac{n-1}2-\frac12$ elsewhere, so its unique maximiser is the point of largest height on the left column; $-x-\varepsilon z$ gives the smallest, and the other sides follow by symmetry.
\end{proof}

For order four the forced vertices number between $4$ and $13$, and in general other points are vertices as well (\Cref{sec:census-4}).

\paragraph{Gems and regular subdivisions.}
\label{sec:subdivisions}
The gem is two regular subdivisions of the grid, by $z$ and by $-z$, glued along the boundary of the grid (with vertical facets over the sides where the entries are not in arithmetic progression); \Cref{fig:construction}e shows the two subdivisions for the Lo Shu.
Let $A$ be the $n\times n$ grid of cell positions.
For a height function $\omega\colon A\to\R$, the \emph{regular subdivision} $\mathcal S(\omega)$ of $A$ is obtained by projecting to the plane the lower faces of $\operatorname{conv}\{(c,\omega(c))\}$, i.e.\ the faces whose outer normals have negative last coordinate; its cells are the sets of grid points whose lifts lie on a common lower face \cite[Ch.~2]{deloera2010triangulations}, \cite[Ch.~7]{gelfand1994discriminants}.
Cells are marked: a cell is a set of grid points, and its convex hull may contain grid points that are not in it.
$\mathcal S(\omega)$ does not change if $\omega$ is multiplied by a positive scalar or an affine function of $(x,y)$ is added to it; the height functions inducing a given subdivision form a relatively open polyhedral cone, its \emph{secondary cone}.
By definition, $\mathcal G(S)$ is the lift of $A$ by the height function $z$ of $S$, and its upper faces are the reflections in $z=0$ of the lower faces of the lift by $-z$.

\begin{proposition}[Gems as pairs of regular subdivisions]
\label{prop:subdivisions}
Let $S$ be an arrangement of order $n\ge2$ that is not a $D_4$-image of the natural square.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Every facet of $\mathcal G(S)$ is a lower facet, an upper facet or a vertical facet. Projection to the plane maps the lower facets onto the maximal cells of $\mathcal S(z)$ and the upper facets onto the maximal cells of $\mathcal S(-z)$.
\item The vertical facets lie in the four planes $x=\pm\frac{n-1}2$, $y=\pm\frac{n-1}2$; the plane over a side of the grid contains a facet if and only if the entries along that side, in cell order, are not an arithmetic progression.
\item $\mathbf p_c$ is a vertex of $\mathcal G(S)$ if and only if $c$ is a vertex of a cell of $\mathcal S(z)$ or of $\mathcal S(-z)$.
\item The face lattice of $\mathcal G(S)$, with vertices labelled by cells, is determined by the pair $(\mathcal S(z),\mathcal S(-z))$, hence by the secondary cones containing $z$ and $-z$. The complement $N+1-S$ exchanges the two subdivisions.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(1) is the definition of $\mathcal S(z)$ and, after reflecting in $z=0$, of $\mathcal S(-z)$.
(2) A vertical facet lies in a plane $\ell(x,y)=\max_A\ell$ for a linear form $\ell$; the maximisers of $\ell$ on the grid are a corner or the $n$ cells of a side, and the face is $2$-dimensional only in the second case and only if the lifts of the side are not collinear, i.e.\ if the heights along the side are not an affine function of the position.
(3) The vertical line through a vertex $\mathbf p_c$ meets $\mathcal G(S)$ in a segment with endpoint $\mathbf p_c$, so $\mathbf p_c$ lies on a lower or an upper face, of which it is a vertex; conversely the vertices of a face are vertices of the polytope.
(4) Let $U$ and $L$ be the upper and lower boundary functions of $\mathcal G(S)$ over $\operatorname{conv}A$.
$U-L$ is concave, non-negative, and positive at the projection of an interior point, hence positive on $\operatorname{int}\operatorname{conv}A$.
So a lower face and an upper face meet only over $\partial\operatorname{conv}A$.
Over a side $e$ of the grid, $\mathcal G(S)$ meets the vertical plane in $\operatorname{conv}\{\mathbf p_c: c\in e\}$.
This polygon is bounded by the restrictions of the two boundary chains to $e$, and it is a vertical facet exactly when these chains differ, i.e.\ (by (2)) when the entries along $e$ are not an arithmetic progression.
A lower or upper facet projects bijectively onto its cell, so its vertices are the lifts of the vertices of the convex hull of the cell.
Hence the marked cells of $\mathcal S(z)$ and $\mathcal S(-z)$, together with the vertical facets, determine every vertex--facet incidence.
The complement has height function $-z$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Lo Shu, continuing \Cref{ex:loshu}]
\label{ex:loshu-subdiv}
Writing each grid point as its entry, $\mathcal S(z)$ has the maximal cells $\{1,2,6\}$, $\{1,3,8\}$, $\{1,2,3,4\}$, and $\mathcal S(-z)$ has $\{2,7,9\}$, $\{4,8,9\}$, $\{6,7,8,9\}$.
The vertical facets are the triangles $\{2,4,9\}$, $\{2,6,7\}$, $\{1,6,8\}$, $\{3,4,8\}$.
The centre point (height $0$) lies in the convex hull of each quadrilateral cell but belongs to no cell: the plane of $\{1,2,3,4\}$ is $z=-x-y-3$, which is $-3<0$ at the centre.
\end{example}

For the $880$ order-four gems, $856$ have four vertical facets, $20$ have three and $4$ have two; the height functions of the $7040$ order-four magic squares induce $417$ distinct regular subdivisions of the $4\times4$ grid up to $D_4$, and for $548$ of the $880$ classes $\mathcal S(z)$ is a triangulation (\texttt{anc/verify\_structure.py}).

\subsection{The census}
\label{sec:census-4}

There are $8$ magic squares of order $3$, all images of the Lo Shu under $D_4$, and $7{,}040$ of order $4$, forming $880$ $D_4$-classes; the order-four squares were listed by Fr\'enicle de Bessy \cite{frenicle1693} and have been recounted by computer \cite{beck2010enumeration}.
Dudeney sorted them into twelve groups according to the pattern of complementary pairs $(a,17-a)$ \cite{dudeney1917amusements}; since the numbering of the groups differs between reproductions of his table, we list the labels used here, for all $7040$ squares together with the number $\#$ of their class, in \texttt{anc/all7040\_dudeney.tsv}.
The number $275{,}305{,}224$ of magic squares of order five up to $D_4$ is due to Schroeppel (1973) \cite{gardner1976}; for order six see \cite{pinn1998number,mino2024order6,trump_howmany}.

For each of the $880$ squares we computed $\mathcal G(S)$ exactly (integer facet inequalities, rational volumes; Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix}).

\paragraph{Vertices, faces and volumes.}
Between $8$ and all $16$ points are vertices; $V=12$ is the most frequent value ($380$ gems), and the mean is $269/22\approx12.23$.
The $39$ $f$-vectors range from $(8,14,8)$ (one gem) to $(16,36,22)$ (nine gems); the most frequent is $(12,26,16)$ ($164$ gems).
The volume takes $155$ distinct values, from $58$ to $292/3$, with mean $84.05$; its denominators are $1,2,3,6$, because for even $n$ all coordinates are half-odd integers, so that $\mathcal G(S)+(\frac12,\frac12,\frac12)$ is a lattice polytope.
The Ehrhart polynomials of these lattice polytopes take $554$ distinct values (Appendix~\ref{sec:ehrhart}).

\paragraph{Combinatorial types.}
Two gems have the same combinatorial type if their vertex--facet incidence graphs are isomorphic by an isomorphism that maps vertices to vertices and facets to facets.
Testing pairs of incidence graphs for isomorphism gives $352$ types (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix}).
By \eqref{eq:complement} the gems of $S$ and $\overline S$ are mirror images, so every type is a union of complement orbits; the $93$ types with one member are self-complementary squares, and of the $201$ types with two members, $177$ are mirror pairs and $24$ consist of two self-complementary squares.
The numbers of types with $1,2,3,\dots$ members are
\[

\text{types} & 93&201&8&20&4&7&4&5&5&1&1&1&1&1
\end{array}
\]
The largest type, with $f=(10,22,14)$ (twelve triangles and two quadrilaterals), has $32$ members.

\begin{proposition}[Congruence classes of the order-four gems; exact computation]
\label{prop:congruence}
Let $S$ and $T$ be magic squares of order four. Then $\mathcal G(S)$ and $\mathcal G(T)$ are congruent if and only if $T\in D_4S\cup D_4\overline S$. There are $616$ congruence classes of gems: $352$ self-complementary classes and $264$ mirror pairs.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
``If'' is \eqref{eq:d4} and \eqref{eq:complement}. For ``only if'': the sorted multiset of pairwise squared distances of the vertex set is a congruence invariant, and it takes $616$ distinct values over the $880$ gems (exact computation). Gems in one complement orbit are congruent, and there are $616$ orbits ($352$ self-complementary classes and $264$ pairs). Hence the congruence classes are exactly the complement orbits.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[The vertex set determines the square]
\label{rem:vertexset}
If $S$, $T$ are magic squares of order four and $\hat g\operatorname{vert}\mathcal G(S)=\operatorname{vert}\mathcal G(T)$ for some $g\in D_4$, then $T=gS$.
This is linear algebra plus a finite check.
By \eqref{eq:d4}, $gS$ and $T$ have the same vertex set, so they have the same entries on the vertex cells and the same set $U$ of other cells.
The ten line sums are linear equations for the entries on $U$, with the $0/1$ matrix $A_U$ of incidences between lines and cells of $U$; if $\operatorname{rank}A_U=|U|$, these entries are determined, and $T=gS$.
This holds for $871$ of the $880$ classes; the rank and everything below are invariant under $D_4$, which permutes the lines.
For the other nine, $\#45$, $\#46$, $\#47$, $\#49$, $\#50$, $\#54$, $\#165$, $\#166$ and $\#672$, we listed every magic square that has the entries of $S$ on the vertex cells of $S$: for five of them $S$ is the only one, and $\#45$, $\#47$, $\#50$, $\#54$ have $1$, $1$, $7$ and $7$ others (for $\#45$ the square $\#109$), but the gems of all these other squares have different vertex sets (\texttt{anc/vertex\_determination.py}).
Since $g^{-1}T$ has the vertex set of $\mathcal G(S)$, it is one of these squares, so $g^{-1}T=S$.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Forced vertices and named squares.}
For order four nothing beyond the forced vertices of \Cref{lem:forced} is a vertex of every gem: the only cells that carry a vertex in all $880$ gems are the four corners, and the only entries that always label a vertex are $1$ and $16$.
In eight gems the point of the entry $2$ is a non-vertex boundary point, for instance for square \#593 below.
The gems of the Lo Shu, of D\"urer's square and of the Siamese square of order $5$ have $8$ of $9$, $12$ of $16$ and $12$ of $25$ points as vertices and volumes $18$, $92$ and $830/3$.

\subsection{Which invariants see the line conditions}
\label{sec:magic-vs-random}

To see whether the gem distinguishes magic squares from other arrangements, we compared four nested families of order four (\Cref{tab:magic-vs-random}; \texttt{anc/magic\_vs\_random.py}).

The counts barely move.
The mean vertex count is $12.06$ for random arrangements, $12.12$ for semi-magic squares and $12.23$ for magic squares ($d=0.14$ against random arrangements, $0.08$ against semi-magic squares).
Self-complementary magic squares have more vertices on average ($12.43$, against $12.09$ for the others; the $48$ associative ones average $11.88$), and they make up $40\%$ of the magic squares but $10\%$ of the semi-magic squares.
This accounts for almost all of the excess over semi-magic squares: over the squares that are not self-complementary, magic and semi-magic squares differ by $0.017\pm0.004$ ($d=0.01$).
It does not account for the excess over random arrangements: the magic squares that are not self-complementary still exceed random arrangements by $0.040\pm0.004$, about a quarter of the excess.
The mean numbers of lower and of upper facets differ by less than $0.08$ between any two of the four families, and the mean number of vertical facets ($3.97$ to $4.00$) by less than $0.03$.

The continuous invariants respond to the hypotheses one at a time.
Vanishing first moments turn the principal axes of the solid towards the coordinate axes (the tilt $(\Sigma_{xz}^2+\Sigma_{yz}^2)^{1/2}$ of the covariance matrix $\Sigma$ of the solid falls from $0.73$ to $0.24$, $d=-1.82$) and add $6.0$ to the mean volume ($d=0.90$), but do not move the solid centroid; the row and column conditions move it towards the origin (from $0.35$ to $0.16$, $d=-1.16$) and add $1.0$ to the volume; and the diagonal conditions add another $1.4$ to the volume ($d=0.21$) and move the centroid further in ($d=-0.62$), also when the self-complementary squares are left out.
A non-trivial isometry occurs for $40\%$ of the magic gems, $10\%$ of the semi-magic gems and almost none of the others, in both families exactly for the self-complementary squares (\Cref{prop:iso4}; for the semi-magic sample by computation, consistent with \Cref{prop:pointset}).

At order five all four families are sampled, $2\cdot10^4$ each, and every hull is certified exactly; the semi-magic squares and the magic squares (distinct $D_4$-classes) come from the annealer of Appendix~\ref{sec:vertexstats}, with other seeds than the larger magic sample of \Cref{tab:vertices}, whose hulls are computed in floating point.
The distribution of the annealer at order five is not known; at order four it hits all $880$ magic classes and reproduces the uniform distribution of the semi-magic squares (Appendix~\ref{sec:vertexstats}).
The mean vertex counts of random arrangements, arrangements with vanishing first moments, semi-magic and magic squares are $15.40$, $15.42$, $15.36$ and $15.39$ (standard errors $0.012$; $|d|\le0.04$; \Cref{tab:vertices} gives $15.38$ for its magic sample), the mean numbers of lower and of upper facets differ by at most $0.16$, and all but $16$ of the $8\cdot10^4$ gems have four vertical facets (the others have three).
The volumes are $250.1$, $260.9$, $265.4$ and $268.5$ ($d=0.22$ for magic against semi-magic squares), and the distance of the solid centroid from the origin is $0.47$, $0.47$, $0.24$ and $0.22$ ($d=-0.10$).

So the magic conditions hardly affect the vertex count; they show in the volume, the position of the solid and the frequency of symmetric squares, and beyond the row and column conditions only by a fraction of a standard deviation.
